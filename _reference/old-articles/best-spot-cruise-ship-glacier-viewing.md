There's no single best spot for glacier viewing, because the best view moves as the day goes on. Go forward and high for the approach, shift to the side rails when the ship turns at the ice, and keep your balcony, if you have one, for your side's turn.

## The deck-by-deck guide

Every ship's layout differs, so check the deck plan in your app the night before. These are the spots that exist on most large ships.

| Spot | Best moment | Why | Downside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bow (if opened) | The approach | Dead ahead, closest to the water at the front | Often closed; fills first |
| Top deck, forward rail | The approach | Highest view straight ahead | Windiest spot on the ship |
| Forward observation lounge | Approach and warming up | Big windows, seats, warm | Glass reflections; no sound |
| Top deck, side rails | The stop and turn | Full panorama with room to move | Crowded at the turn |
| Promenade deck | Calving and wildlife | Close to the water, quieter | Lower view of peaks |
| Your balcony | Your side's turn | Private, warm cabin behind you | One side only |
| Aft open deck | Leaving the glacier | Ice and ice floes in the wake | Some ships have little aft space |

## How to plan the day, in order

1. **Check the timing the night before.** The daily programme or app gives the expected arrival time, though it can shift with ice and weather.
2. **Dress before you go out.** Glacier air is near freezing even in July. A hat, gloves and a windproof layer make the difference between ten minutes and two hours outside; the [Alaska cruise dress code](/articles/alaska-cruise-dress-code/) has what people actually wear.
3. **Go forward an hour early.** As the ship enters the fjord, the forward rails of the highest open deck are the place to be. If your ship opens the bow for glacier day, an announcement will tell you.
4. **Move up and to the side for the turn.** At the face, the ship stops and slowly rotates, giving each side its turn. The top deck's side rails let you follow the glacier as the ship turns.
5. **Duck to your balcony for your side's turn.** If you have one, this is its best half hour.
6. **Finish at the stern.** As the ship leaves, the glacier shrinks behind you, and the aft deck gives a good last look across the floating ice.

## Watching for calving

Calving, when a chunk of ice breaks off the face, is what everyone waits for. Watch the face rather than your screen: you will usually see the ice fall before you hear it, because sound takes a few seconds to cross the water. The crack and rumble are worth being outside for.

> **Watch out:** rail space is shared. Take your photos, then step back and let someone else in, especially children. Tripods and selfie sticks are unpopular in a crowd, and drones are banned on almost every ship, as explained in [cruise ship drone rules](/articles/cruise-ship-drone-photography-rules/).

## What to bring on deck

- Binoculars, for seals hauled out on the ice and mountain goats on the cliffs.
- A thermos or a hot drink from the buffet.
- Hand warmers, which also help keep phone and camera batteries alive in the cold.
- A fully charged camera; settings for white-on-white scenes are in [Alaska cruise photography tips](/articles/best-alaska-cruise-photography/).

## Does it depend on which glacier?

The approach to Dawes Glacier in Endicott Arm is long and narrow, so the forward rails matter most there. Hubbard is so large that the side rails during the turn are the main event, and Glacier Bay is an all-day affair with several glaciers along the way. ## Picking the sailing with the glacier you want

Not every Alaska itinerary visits Glacier Bay, because park permits limit which ships go in. If a particular glacier matters to you, choose the sailing for it deliberately, then plan your deck time around the arrival.
