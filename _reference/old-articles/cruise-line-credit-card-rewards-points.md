For most Canadian cruisers, the best card is not a cruise line card at all. It is a travel rewards card with no foreign transaction fee. The cruise line cards you read about online are mainly US products, and on a US-dollar onboard bill, avoiding the conversion fee usually saves more than any reward earns.

## Can Canadians get a cruise line credit card?
Mostly no. The well-known cruise line cards, from lines such as Royal Caribbean, Carnival and Norwegian, are issued by US banks for US residents. Reviews praising their onboard credit or bonus points rarely mention that Canadian addresses are generally not eligible.

If a Canadian version does appear for your favourite line, judge it like any other card: annual fee, earn rate, how the rewards are redeemed, and whether it charges foreign transaction fees.

## The three kinds of card worth comparing
| Card type | What it does well | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| No foreign transaction fee card | Saves about 2.5% on USD fares, onboard bills and port spending | Rewards may be modest; some carry an annual fee |
| Flexible travel points card | Points usable on cruises, flights and hotels | Usually charges 2.5% on foreign currency |
| Airline card (Aeroplan, WestJet) | Strong value on flights to Vancouver or Florida | Weaker value when used for the cruise itself |

Many regular cruisers end up with two: a no-FX-fee card for anything in US dollars and a points card for Canadian-dollar spending, including the fare if it is booked in CAD.

## Why the foreign transaction fee matters most
Most Canadian credit cards add about 2.5% to purchases in foreign currency. On a cruise, that hits the fare if you book in US dollars, every drink and spa treatment on your onboard account, prepaid excursions and port shopping.

> **Quick math:** a couple spends US$1,200 on board during a 7-night cruise. A standard card adds 2.5%, about US$30 or C$41. If that card earns 1 point per dollar worth about one cent, the rewards come to roughly C$16. The fee wipes out the reward more than twice over. A no-FX-fee card keeps that C$41.

The same logic applies to the fare. A US$5,000 fare charged in USD on a standard card costs about US$125 extra, roughly C$170. Booking in Canadian dollars, where offered, avoids that entirely. [Paying in CAD vs USD](/articles/paying-cad-vs-usd-cruise/) covers the options.

## Points: earning vs redeeming
### Earning on cruise spending
Cruise fares usually count as travel purchases, which some Canadian cards reward at a higher rate than everyday spending. Check whether your card's travel category includes cruise lines and travel agencies.

### Redeeming for a cruise
Flexible programs often let you apply points against a travel charge at a fixed value, or book through the card's own travel service. Airline points generally give their best value on flights. The detail is in [booking a cruise with points vs cash](/articles/cruise-points-vs-cash-booking/), and the common winner is points for the flights, cash for the cruise.

> **Canadian tip:** many premium Canadian cards include trip cancellation and interruption insurance when you charge the trip to the card, but emergency medical cover is often limited by trip length and age, with cover commonly shrinking or ending for older travellers. Read the certificate before relying on it, and see [travel insurance for Canadian cruisers](/articles/travel-insurance-cruise-canadians/).

## How to choose your setup
1. **Check whether you already have a no-FX-fee card.** If not, that is your first priority if you cruise regularly.
2. **Decide what currency you will book in.** CAD bookings can go on your best points card; USD bookings belong on the no-FX-fee card.
3. **Link the no-FX-fee card to your onboard account** at check-in. [How cruise cards work on board](/articles/cruise-cards-how-you-pay-onboard/) explains the setup.
4. **Check your card's travel insurance** against your age, trip length and trip cost.
5. **Keep cruise loyalty separate.** Cruise line loyalty tiers come from nights sailed, not the card you use. See [cruise loyalty programs worth chasing](/articles/cruise-loyalty-programs-worth-chasing/).

## Who might still want a cruise line card
- Canadians with a US address and US credit history who sail one line several times a year.
- Travellers who would use the specific perks, such as onboard credit or upgrades, every year.

For everyone else, the flexible approach wins: one fee-free card for US dollars, one strong points card for Canadian spending.

## The short version
Before chasing points, stop paying 2.5% on every US-dollar charge. Once that is sorted, earn flexible points on your Canadian-dollar spending and spend airline points on getting to the ship.
