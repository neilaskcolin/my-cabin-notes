A cruise with your grown children works best when everyone has their own cabin, the money conversation happens before the booking, and the itinerary leaves room for people to go their own way. Think of it as a trip among adults who happen to be related.

## Cabins: separate and nearby
Adult children, especially those bringing a partner, want their own space. Book cabins on the same deck and ideally side by side, so people can knock on each other's doors without anyone sharing a bathroom. Balconies next to each other are a bonus: some lines can open the divider between neighbouring balconies on request.

Different cabin grades are fine. Parents might book a balcony while a son and his girlfriend take an inside cabin a few doors down. [Booking multiple cabins under one name](/articles/booking-multiple-cabins-under-one-name/) explains how to keep the reservations linked so you can dine and transfer together.

## Who pays? Have the conversation first
Assumptions about money cause more family-trip tension than anything that happens on board. Before anyone books, pick a model that suits your family.

| Approach | How it works | Works well when |
|---|---|---|
| Parents' gift | Parents pay fares, maybe flights too | It's a milestone, such as a big anniversary or retirement |
| Shared split | Parents pay the cruise, children pay flights and onboard extras | Children are earning but budgets are tighter |
| Everyone pays their own | Each couple books and pays separately | Incomes are similar and independence matters |
| Parents top up | Everyone pays a base cabin, parents fund upgrades or excursions | Siblings have very different incomes |

Be specific about the extras: gratuities, drink packages, wifi and excursions add up fast. On many lines, if one adult in a cabin buys an unlimited drink package, everyone of drinking age in that cabin must buy it too, so let each couple make that call for themselves. [Are drink packages worth it](/articles/drink-packages-worth-it-first-cruise/) has the maths.

> **Good to know:** most lines set the drinking age at 21 on sailings from North American ports, including Vancouver. A 19-year-old who drinks legally in BC or a 20-year-old from Alberta may be refused at the bar.

## Dining and time together
Flexible or "anytime" dining usually suits adult families better than fixed seating, because not everyone wants to eat at 6 p.m. every night. Agree on two or three family dinners, perhaps the first night, a formal night and the last evening, and book a specialty restaurant for one of them. [How dining works on a modern cruise ship](/articles/how-dining-works-cruise/) covers the options.

### Anchor moments, not a schedule
Pick a handful of shared highlights: one excursion everyone wants, a sail-away drink on someone's balcony, a family photo. Leave the rest free. Adult children may want late nights, quiet mornings or a port day on their own, and that's a sign the trip is working, not failing.

## Including partners properly
Sons- and daughters-in-law, or newer partners, should be part of the planning from the start. Ask their preferences on dates, excursions and budget directly rather than through your child. A partner who feels like a guest at someone else's family holiday will have a much harder week.

## Planning it in order
1. Ask everyone for their available dates and vacation days before choosing a sailing.
2. Agree on the payment approach and a rough budget per couple.
3. Pick an itinerary with something for each couple, with flights everyone can manage from where they live.
4. Book cabins together, then link the reservations.
5. Share a simple plan: two or three shared dinners and one group excursion.
6. Let each couple book their own extras.

> **Example:** parents in Edmonton with one child in Toronto and one in Vancouver might choose a 7-night Alaska sailing from Vancouver in June. The Vancouver couple has no flight, the Toronto couple flies direct, and the parents take a short hop. Three cabins in a row, parents covering fares as a 35th anniversary gift, and everyone else covering flights and onboard spending.

## A trip your grown children look forward to
Get the cabins and the money sorted early, plan less than you think you should, and treat partners as full members of the group.
