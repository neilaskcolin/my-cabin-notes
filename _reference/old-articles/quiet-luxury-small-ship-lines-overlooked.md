The quieter end of luxury cruising sits with boutique lines most Canadians never see advertised: Windstar, SeaDream, Star Clippers, Ponant and Azamara among them. Their ships carry roughly 100 to 700 guests, reach smaller harbours and often stay later in port. Prices can undercut the headline luxury names, but you have to read the inclusions carefully.

## Which small-ship lines get overlooked?
Guest numbers and inclusions below are approximate and change over time, so confirm them for your ship and sailing.

| Line | Rough ship size | Style | Typical regions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Windstar | About 150–340 guests | Relaxed yachts and sail-assisted ships, casual dress | Mediterranean, Caribbean, Tahiti |
| SeaDream Yacht Club | About 110 guests | Mega-yacht feel, drinks and gratuities included | Caribbean, Mediterranean |
| Star Clippers | About 170–230 guests | Real tall ships under sail, informal | Mediterranean, Caribbean, Asia |
| Ponant | About 180–260 guests | French style, open bar, also does expeditions | Europe, polar regions, worldwide |
| Azamara | About 700 guests | Mid-size, long port stays and overnights | Europe, Asia, worldwide |

None of these is a mass-market line, and none is trying to be a floating resort. There are no waterslides, few if any production shows, and a lot of time in port.

## What "overlooked" really means
These lines do not spend on TV campaigns or big-ship features. They rely heavily on repeat guests, so standards tend to be what keeps them in business. For Canadians, the catch is mostly awareness: few travel agencies push them, and fares rarely appear in the seat-sale emails that bigger brands send.

### What you usually get
- **Scale:** dinner without reservations, no queues at tenders, and crew who learn your name.
- **Itineraries:** smaller ports bigger ships cannot reach, and sometimes overnights or late departures.
- **Inclusions:** on some lines, drinks, gratuities and wifi are in the fare. On others they are extra.
- **Atmosphere:** adult and low-key. Families are rare, though children are not always barred.

### What you give up
- Choice of restaurants and evening entertainment is limited.
- Medical facilities are smaller, which matters on remote itineraries.
- Smaller ships move more in open water.
- Fewer sailings mean less flexibility on dates.

> **Quick math:** compare the full daily cost, not the fare. A premium big-ship balcony at US$250 per person per night, plus a drink package around US$80, gratuities near US$18 and wifi near US$20, comes to roughly US$370 (C$500–520). An inclusive boutique fare of US$450–550 a night (roughly C$620–770) is then a much smaller jump than it first looks.

## How this differs from expedition cruising
Both use small ships, but the goals differ. Small-ship luxury is about comfort, food and long evenings in places like Santorini, Dubrovnik or the Grenadines. Expedition cruising is about reaching wild places, with Zodiac landings, naturalists and early starts. If wildlife is the draw, see [small-ship lines for wildlife and nature lovers](/articles/best-small-ship-lines-wildlife-nature/) or [Antarctica expedition cruise costs](/articles/antarctica-expedition-cruise-cost-worth-it/).

## Who these lines suit, and who they don't
They suit couples who have outgrown mega-ships, enjoy wine and conversation more than shows, and want to spend their days ashore. They suit people who would rather have a small, excellent restaurant than eight average ones.

They are a poorer fit for anyone who wants nightlife, a casino, lots of dining variety or a guaranteed calm ride. For a sense of what the top luxury brands add, compare [what the extra spend buys on luxury lines](/articles/luxury-lines-compared-extra-spend/).

## The Canadian angle
Most boutique lines price in US dollars, so an inclusive fare is useful for Canadians: more of the trip is paid up front, at a known exchange rate, rather than on an onboard account that keeps adding up. Many sail Europe and the Caribbean, so plan for a transatlantic or Florida flight; Some lines come closer to home in certain seasons; Ponant, for example, has run Great Lakes and St. Lawrence sailings, so check current schedules.

Your provincial health plan will not cover you abroad or at sea, and small ships on remote routes make strong medical and evacuation cover more important, not less.

## Questions to ask before booking
1. Which drinks, gratuities and wifi are included, and are any limited to certain brands or hours?
2. Is the ship docking or tendering in each port?
3. What is the dress code in the evening?
4. How old is the ship, and when was it last refurbished?
5. What are the cancellation terms? Small-ship lines sometimes have stricter ones.

A licensed cruise consultant who books these lines regularly can help compare them, since they rarely appear side by side online.
