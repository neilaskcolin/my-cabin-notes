Norwegian is the odd one out among the big lines: there is no fixed limit on how much wine you can bring, but you pay corkage on every bottle as it comes aboard. Beer, spirits and liqueurs are not allowed at all.

## What alcohol can you bring on Norwegian Cruise Line?
Wine and champagne, carried on at embarkation, by guests of legal drinking age (21 on Norwegian's North American sailings). Bottles should be sealed and packed in your hand luggage rather than checked bags, so they go through the security scan with you.

Beer, hard seltzers, spirits and liqueurs are refused in any quantity.

## How the corkage fee works
At recent policy Norwegian charges about US$15 per 750 ml bottle, scaling up for larger formats, and posts it to your onboard account when the bottle is logged at security. Most lines charge corkage only when you open a bottle in a restaurant. On Norwegian, the fee is paid up front, so it is the same whether you drink the bottle on your balcony or hand it to a waiter at Cagney's.

> **Quick math:** a C$25 bottle from home plus US$15 corkage (roughly C$20–21) comes to about C$45–46 on board. That is good value for a wine you love, and a poor deal for an ordinary bottle you could match from the ship's list.

### Bringing wine aboard, step by step
1. Wrap each bottle in clothing or a padded sleeve and put it in your carry-on.
2. At the terminal security check, the bottles are scanned and logged against your cabin.
3. Check the Norwegian app or your onboard account on day one to make sure the corkage count is right.
4. Ask your stateroom attendant for wine glasses and a corkscrew if the cabin doesn't have them.

> **Canadian tip:** for Alaska sailings, grocery stores in Washington state sell wine, which is handy if you fly from Calgary or Edmonton into Seattle and don't want bottles in your luggage. For a Canada Place departure, pick one up in Vancouver the day before.

## Spirits, beer and bottles bought in port
Anything prohibited that turns up at security is tagged and held, then returned on the last night or as you leave the ship. Assume any bottle bought ashore, from Caribbean rum to a souvenir liqueur in Juneau, will be stored the same way. Duty-free purchases from the ship's shops are delivered at the end too, as [onboard duty-free shopping](/articles/cruise-shopping-onboard-duty-free/) explains.

On the way home, Canada's personal exemption after 48 hours away is 1.5 litres of wine, or 1.14 litres of spirits, or 8.5 litres of beer, per adult. Anything above that is taxed at the border.

## Does Free at Sea make bringing wine pointless?
For many guests, yes. Norwegian's Free at Sea promotion often includes the Unlimited Open Bar package for the first two guests in a cabin, and that covers wine by the glass up to a per-drink price cap. If that is on your fare, bringing wine only makes sense for a particular bottle you want with dinner or for a celebration. Without the package, compare the corkage maths above with [Norwegian's drink package price](/articles/norwegian-drink-package-price/) before deciding.

## How Norwegian compares
| Line | Wine you can bring | Corkage (recent) |
|---|---|---|
| **Norwegian** | No fixed limit | ~$15 per bottle, paid at boarding |
| [Royal Caribbean](/articles/royal-caribbean-alcohol-policy/) | 2 bottles per stateroom | ~$15 in bars and restaurants |
| [Carnival](/articles/carnival-alcohol-policy/) | 1 bottle per adult (21+) | ~$15 in bars and restaurants |

Norwegian is the most generous on quantity and the strictest on cost: no cap on bottles, but corkage on all of them at the gangway. Royal Caribbean allows two bottles per stateroom and Carnival one per adult, with corkage only if you open them in a bar or restaurant.
