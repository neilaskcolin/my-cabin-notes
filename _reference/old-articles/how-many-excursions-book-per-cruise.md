Two or three excursions on a 7-night cruise is the sweet spot for most first-timers. Booking a tour at every port sounds efficient, but by day five the early starts and bus queues usually outweigh the fun, and the bill grows fast.

## Why one at every port backfires
Back-to-back tour days mean early alarms, meeting points and limited time to enjoy the ship you paid for. Port days also come in clusters, so a week can easily include four tour days in a row. Leaving one or two open gives you a lie-in, a walk at your own pace, or a pool with half the passengers ashore.

The cost adds up too. At typical prices of US$70–150 per person for a Caribbean tour, and US$150–250 or more in Alaska, a tour at every port can double your onboard spend.

## A rule of thumb by itinerary
Here is how the advice changes by the trips Canadians book most. Counts are a starting point per person.

| Itinerary | Typical ports | Book | Do on your own |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7-night Alaska from Vancouver or Seattle | Ketchikan, Juneau, Skagway, plus Victoria or Sitka | 2–3 | Ketchikan's Creek Street, Skagway's main street, Victoria in the evening |
| 7-night Western Caribbean from Florida | Cozumel, Roatán, Costa Maya, Belize | 2 | Beach days and waterfront walks |
| 7-night Eastern Caribbean from Florida | St. Thomas, St. Maarten, private island | 1–2 | Beaches by taxi, the private island |
| 3- to 5-night Bahamas | Nassau, private island | 0–1 | Nassau on foot, beach at the island |
| 7-night Mediterranean | Mostly cities | 3–4 | One or two cities by train or on foot |

The Mediterranean is the exception: ports are spread out and full of sights, so more guided days make sense.

> **Example:** a Regina couple on a 7-night Alaska sailing might book whale watching in Juneau and the White Pass railway in Skagway, then walk Ketchikan using the [free Ketchikan walking plan](/articles/ketchikan-free-walking-plan/). That is two tours each, about US$600–900 per couple, with one port day free.

## How to choose which ports get a tour
1. List what is unique to each port: whales in Juneau, a historic railway in Skagway, Mayan ruins near Costa Maya.
2. Book only what you genuinely cannot do on foot or by taxi.
3. Skip repeat activities. If you snorkel in Cozumel, you may not need another snorkel trip in Roatán.
4. Check the port times. A short call of five or six hours leaves little room for a long tour.
5. Note which ports use tenders, since queues eat into your day. See [how tenders work](/articles/cruise-ship-tenders-explained/).
6. Book the headline tours early, because whale watching and glacier flights sell out. [When to book cruise excursions](/articles/best-time-book-cruise-excursions/) covers timing.

For Alaska specifically, [are Alaska shore excursions worth it](/articles/alaska-shore-excursions-worth-it/) and [Juneau excursions ranked](/articles/juneau-shore-excursions-ranked/) help narrow the list.

## Ship tours or independent?
Ship tours cost more, but the ship waits if the tour runs late. Independent tours are often cheaper and smaller, but you are responsible for being back on time. The full trade-off is in [booking through the ship vs independently](/articles/cruise-excursion-through-ship-vs-independent/).

> **Watch out:** all-aboard times are on ship's time, which may differ from local time in some ports. Set your watch by the ship, not your phone.

## Adjusting for your style
Energetic sightseers can push to four on a port-heavy week, and relaxers may book just one. Families with young children often do best with one booked activity and beach or walking days around it. ## Planning the right mix
Look at your itinerary port by port, pick the two or three experiences that only that trip offers, and leave the rest open. You will come home with the highlights and enough energy to enjoy them.
