Getting fifteen relatives to agree on a restaurant is hard enough. A cruise solves a lot of the reunion problem: one venue, something for every generation at the same time, and nobody driving anyone home. Here's how to plan one month by month.

## Month 10–12: pick the ship and the season
For a reunion, the ship matters more than the destination. You want kids clubs for the little ones, teen space that isn't embarrassing, quiet adult areas for the grandparents, and enough dining variety that a picky eight-year-old and a foodie uncle both eat well. Mainstream ships with strong family programmes usually beat premium lines here. [The best cruise lines for family reunions](/articles/best-cruise-lines-family-reunions/) ranks them by age mix.

Then pick the season. July and August suit families with school-age kids everywhere in Canada, and Alaska from Vancouver is easy to reach from BC and Alberta. For March break, check every household's dates first, because break weeks vary by province.

## Month 8–10: open the group block
Book as one group, not fifteen separate reservations. A group block holds cabins at a set rate, unlocks the organiser's free berth at about eight cabins, and lets you place cabins near each other before the good ones go. The mechanics are in [how the 8-cabin rule works](/articles/group-cruise-8-cabin-rule/).

Think about layout too. Connecting cabins suit young families, while adult siblings may prefer the same corridor with their own doors. The options are compared in [connecting cabins vs one big cabin](/articles/connecting-cabins-one-big-cabin-families/).

Set a firm date for each household to confirm. Group blocks can release unclaimed cabins back to the cruise line by a set date.

## Month 6–8: money, kept separate but transparent
Money causes more reunion friction than anything else, so sort it early. Each household pays for its own cabin and onboard spending. Agree separately on any shared costs, such as a big group dinner, a private excursion or a beach cabana, and collect that pot on its own. Nobody should get a bill that was never theirs.

> **Example:** a Winnipeg family of 22 across seven households could agree on a C$150 per adult shared pot to cover one private cocktail hour, a group photo session and a beach day. One cousin keeps the money and the receipts, and the rest is up to each household.

## Month 3–6: shared tables and special needs
Reserve one or two large tables in the main dining room for at least a few nights. That table becomes the real centre of the reunion, more than any excursion. Flag dietary needs, high chairs and mobility needs when you book. Seating a big group is covered in [how to seat a group of 20 together](/articles/group-dining-cruises-20-people/).

## Month 1–2: excursions and documents
Not every generation wants the same shore day. Book one big group excursion everyone attends, and leave other port days open for smaller groups: the teenagers on the zipline, the grandparents on the walking tour, everyone back together for dinner.

Then work through the documents:

1. Check every passport, including children's, is valid well beyond the return date.
2. If any child is travelling without both parents, carry a signed consent letter. The Government of Canada recommends one.
3. Make sure every household has travel medical insurance, because provincial health plans cover little at sea.
4. Share the boarding times, cabin numbers and the dinner plan in one family message.

## Who does what
One lead organiser burns out. Share the jobs:
- **Lead organiser:** holds the group block and the deadlines.
- **Money keeper:** collects the shared pot and keeps receipts.
- **Dining captain:** manages table requests and dietary notes.
- **Excursion lead:** polls interest and books the group day.
- **Kids' coordinator:** tracks kids club sign-ups and babysitting plans.

> **Canadian tip:** set up a family group chat early, and agree that onboard messaging will be patchy. Many Canadian phone plans charge steep rates at sea, so pick a meeting spot and time each evening instead of relying on texts.

## Running fifteen people through one booking
Group blocks, cabin placement, table sizes and staggered deposits from different households are routine work for whoever holds the booking. If you'd rather not be that person, a licensed cruise consultant can manage it at no cost above individual fares.
