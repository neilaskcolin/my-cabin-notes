A first Alaska cruise is a scenery holiday: glaciers, forested channels, whales and small frontier towns, seen as much from the deck as from shore. Get five things right and the rest takes care of itself: the route, the month, a warm wardrobe, a valid passport and travel insurance.

## How is Alaska different from other cruises?

If your only cruise so far was in the Caribbean, recalibrate. There are no beach days, and the pool deck is quiet unless the sun is out. The highlights are often on the ship itself: the glacier day, scenic cruising through narrow channels, a humpback surfacing beside the ship at dinner.

Days start early and run long. Ports often open at 7 a.m., and in June and July it stays light past 10 p.m., so many people end up more tired than on a sun holiday, in a good way.

## A first-timer's planning timeline

1. **9–12 months out:** choose your sailing and book, especially for July or a specific cabin. Check everyone's passport expiry the same day.
2. **6–9 months out:** buy travel insurance soon after your deposit, so cancellation cover starts early.
3. **4–6 months out:** book flights to Vancouver. Seat sales from Calgary, Edmonton, Winnipeg and Toronto come and go, and July fills early.
4. **As soon as your line allows:** book the excursions you care about. Whale watching, helicopter trips and the White Pass railway sell out on summer sailings.
5. **2–3 weeks out:** complete online check-in, print luggage tags and pack layers.
6. **The day before:** fly to Vancouver and stay near Canada Place.

> **Watch out:** a same-day flight is the most common way first-timers miss an Alaska ship. One delayed morning flight from the Prairies is enough. See [flying the day before vs same day](/articles/fly-day-before-cruise/).

## What to pack and wear

Even in July, Alaska port towns are often in the mid-teens Celsius, and it feels near freezing on deck at a glacier. Pack the way you would for a cool, wet spring week: a waterproof shell, a fleece or light down layer, a warm hat, gloves and shoes that can handle rain. Ketchikan is one of the wettest towns in North America, so the rain jacket is not optional. Evenings on board are relaxed, as covered in [what people actually wear in Alaska](/articles/alaska-cruise-dress-code/).

## Passport, money and phones

- **Passport:** you enter the US through preclearance at Canada Place before boarding. A valid passport is the simplest document; see [passports for an Alaska cruise from Vancouver](/articles/passport-alaska-cruise-vancouver/).
- **Money:** onboard accounts and port shops use US dollars. A no-foreign-transaction-fee credit card saves about 2.5% on every purchase.
- **Phones:** your Canadian carrier's US roaming plan generally works in port, but the ship's own network at sea can be very expensive. Put your phone in airplane mode between ports.

> **Canadian tip:** your provincial health plan covers little or nothing of a US hospital bill or a visit to the ship's medical centre. Read [cruise insurance for Canadians](/articles/travel-insurance-cruise-canadians/) before you buy, and declare any pre-existing conditions honestly.

## Wildlife: realistic expectations

Most Alaska sailings deliver bald eagles in every port and humpbacks somewhere along the way, but nothing is guaranteed. Bears are the hardest to see from a ship, and seeing one reliably usually means a dedicated tour. Binoculars make a bigger difference than any camera; keep a pair by the window at breakfast.

## Your first week, in short

Pick the Inside Passage round trip, sail between late May and mid-September, book a balcony if the budget allows, and choose one excursion per big port.
