"Fare lock" and "price guarantee" get used for three different things, and the protection each one gives is very different. Before you rely on one, check which type you have, when it ends, and whether any refund comes as cash or onboard credit.

## The three kinds of price protection
| | Courtesy hold | Formal price guarantee | Manual repricing |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Keeps a cabin at a quoted fare before you pay | A written promise tied to a fare or channel | Asking for a lower fare after booking |
| When | A day to a few days before deposit | From booking until a set date, often final payment | Before final payment, while the lower fare is bookable |
| Cost | Free | Sometimes built into a fare with other trade-offs | Free on most refundable fares |
| What you get | Time to decide | The difference, often as onboard credit | A lower fare or better promotion |
| Who acts | You pay before it expires | Depends on the terms; sometimes automatic, often you claim | You or your agent must ask |

### Courtesy holds
This is what most people mean by a fare lock. Many lines and agents can hold a cabin for a short time, so you can check flights or talk it over. When the hold expires, the cabin goes back on sale and the fare can change. It is useful during a sale, when the cabin you want might not be there tomorrow.

### Formal guarantees
Some fares come with written price protection. Carnival's Early Saver fare, for example, has offered onboard credit if the price drops before final payment, in exchange for a non-refundable deposit and other conditions; confirm the current terms. Some agencies and online sellers run their own price-drop promises too.

### Manual repricing
On most refundable fares you can ask for a lower price before final payment, with no named program at all. [Price drops after booking](/articles/price-drop-after-booking-cruise/) covers each line's approach, and [asking for price protection](/articles/cruise-price-protection-asking-for-it/) gives a script.

> **Good to know:** onboard credit is not the same as a refund. It can only be spent on the ship, usually expires at the end of the sailing, and is denominated in US dollars. A US$150 credit is worth roughly C$200–210 only if you would have spent that much on board anyway.

## Questions to ask before relying on a guarantee
1. **Which fares qualify?** Group rates, non-refundable fares and special promotions are often excluded.
2. **When does it end?** Most stop at final payment.
3. **What must match?** Usually the same ship, date, cabin category and promotion.
4. **How is it paid?** Cash refund, reduced balance or onboard credit.
5. **Do I have to claim it?** Some programs need you to spot the drop and submit a request within a set time.
6. **Do I lose anything by switching?** Moving onto a new promotion can remove perks from the old one.

## Which is best?
For most Canadians booking months ahead, a refundable fare plus regular price checks gives the most flexibility. A formal guarantee is a handy extra if it comes with a fare you wanted anyway, but it is rarely worth accepting a non-refundable deposit just to get it. [Refundable vs non-refundable fares](/articles/cruise-fare-classes-refundable/) explains that trade-off. Setting up [cruise price alerts](/articles/how-to-set-cruise-price-alerts/) helps you spot drops either way.

## The bottom line
A courtesy hold buys time, a guarantee buys a promise, and repricing buys you a lower fare if you ask. Use the hold to secure the cabin, book a refundable fare, and keep watching the price until final payment.
