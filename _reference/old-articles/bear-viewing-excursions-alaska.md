Bears are one of the most wanted sightings on an Alaska cruise, and one of the least likely to happen by chance. Timing is the biggest factor, followed by which port you book from. Get both right and the odds become very good.

## When to go: follow the salmon
Bears gather where salmon run. In Southeast Alaska, the big runs that pull bears to streams build from mid-July and peak in August, with later runs into September. That makes late July to early September the prime window.

In May and June, bears are out of hibernation but spread across hillsides and beaches eating plants. Sightings happen, but you can't plan around them. If bears are a priority, book an August sailing; [Alaska weather month by month](/articles/alaska-cruise-weather-by-month/) shows what else that month brings.

## Choosing the right trip
- **Want a near-certain sighting on a budget?** Sitka's rescue centre guarantees bears, though they live in a large enclosure rather than in the wild.
- **Want wild bears without a floatplane?** Icy Strait Point's tours and Juneau's Steep Creek are your best bets in late summer.
- **Want the classic "bear catching salmon" scene?** A floatplane trip to a stream or observatory in August gives the best odds and the closest views.
- **Doing a cruisetour?** A fly-out from Anchorage to Katmai, famous for bears fishing at waterfalls, is a strong add-on. See [Alaska cruisetours explained](/articles/alaska-cruisetours-denali-rail/).

> **Watch out:** floatplanes don't fly in low cloud, and Southeast Alaska weather cancels plenty of trips. Book through the ship or an operator with a clear refund policy, and don't plan a fly-out on a port day that leaves no buffer.

## What a fly-out day looks like
Most floatplane trips last three to five hours. You fly 20 to 60 minutes over forest and fjords, land on water, then walk with a guide to a platform or viewing area above a stream. Expect to spend one to two hours watching. Seats are limited, often to a handful of passengers per plane, so peak-season trips sell out early.

Weight limits and sometimes weight-based fares apply on small aircraft. Floatplane boarding involves stepping onto a float, which isn't suitable for everyone; [accessible excursions port by port](/articles/alaska-cruise-accessible-excursions-port-by-port/) covers alternatives.

> **Good to know:** guides keep groups together and at a safe distance, and viewing sites have rules about noise, food and movement. Follow them exactly. Never try to approach a bear for a better photo.

## Getting the photos
Bring a zoom lens or a phone with good optical zoom, since even close viewing sites keep you well back. Overcast light near streams is dim, so steady your camera on a railing. [Alaska cruise photography from deck](/articles/best-alaska-cruise-photography/) has settings that carry over.

## Setting realistic expectations
Even in peak season, wild bears are never guaranteed. A good operator in August gives strong odds, and the floatplane flight, the rainforest and the eagles that gather at the same streams make the trip worthwhile regardless.
