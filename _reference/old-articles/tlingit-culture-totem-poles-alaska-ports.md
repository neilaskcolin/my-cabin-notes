Southeast Alaska is Tlingit country, with Haida and Tsimshian communities in the south, and every major cruise port sits on land with thousands of years of history behind it. With a little planning you can spend part of a port day learning from that culture directly, not just photographing it.

## What totem poles actually are
Totem poles are not religious idols. They are carved records: clan crests, family histories, stories, memorials and, occasionally, public rebukes. Each figure has meaning tied to a specific clan and event, which is why a guided visit or good signage adds so much.

Many original poles were left to weather in abandoned villages in the early 20th century. Some were later moved to museums, and many standing poles today are carefully carved replicas or new works by living artists. Both are authentic in their own way, and carvers continue the tradition today.

## Which ports are best for Indigenous culture?
| Port | Where to go | Getting there | Time needed |

### Ketchikan: the pole capital
Ketchikan is the strongest single port for this. The Totem Heritage Center, an easy walk from the docks, protects original 19th-century poles brought in from abandoned villages. Saxman, a few kilometres south, has a large pole park and a carving centre where you may see work in progress. Totem Bight, further north, pairs poles with a reconstructed clan house in a forest setting.

### Juneau and Sitka
Juneau's Sealaska Heritage Institute runs a downtown centre with exhibits and, often, artists at work, and its totem trail along the waterfront features poles representing Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian clans. Sitka is where Tlingit and Russian colonial history collide most visibly, and the national historical park's forest trail of poles is one of the loveliest short walks on any Alaska itinerary. ### Icy Strait Point and Glacier Bay
At Icy Strait Point, revenue flows to a Native-owned corporation, and performances are presented by the community itself (more in [Icy Strait Point: what's actually there](/articles/icy-strait-point-whats-there/)). Glacier Bay is the Huna Tlingit ancestral homeland. On many sailings, park rangers board for the day, sometimes alongside a Huna Tlingit cultural interpreter, so stay near the ship's commentary.

> **Canadian tip:** start before you sail. Vancouver's Museum of Anthropology at UBC and the totem poles at Brockton Point in Stanley Park give you a grounding in Northwest Coast art. Tlingit territory also extends into northern BC and Yukon, including the Carcross area that some Skagway rail-and-road tours visit.

## How to visit respectfully
- Choose community-owned or Indigenous-led tours where they exist.
- Don't touch, lean on or climb poles, even weathered ones.
- Ask before photographing people, especially dancers and carvers.
- Listen to the story behind a pole rather than treating it as a backdrop.
- Treat clan houses and performance spaces as you would a place of worship.

## Buying Alaska Native art
Souvenir shops are full of "Native-style" pieces made elsewhere. Look for Alaska's Silver Hand label, which identifies work by Alaska Native artists, and buy directly from cultural centres or artists where you can. A "Made in Alaska" sticker alone does not mean Native-made.

> **Watch out:** CBSA may ask about items made from animal products, such as ivory, bone or fur trim. Keep receipts and any documentation the seller gives you, and declare them when you return to Canada.
