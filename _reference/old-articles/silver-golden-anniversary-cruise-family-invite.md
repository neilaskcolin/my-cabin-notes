A family cruise for a 25th or 50th anniversary works best when it is booked as a group 12 to 18 months ahead, with an honest early conversation about money. The ship handles the rest: everyone has their own cabin, meals are easy to share, and there is something for each generation.

## Start with the money conversation
Before choosing a ship, decide who pays for what. This one conversation prevents most family friction later. For more on adult-child dynamics, see [cruising with adult children](/articles/cruise-with-adult-children-parents/).

| Approach | Couple pays | Families pay | Works well when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full gift | Cabins, taxes, gratuities, sometimes flights | Personal extras | The couple wants everyone there, whatever their budget |
| Shared | Cabins and the celebration dinner | Flights, drinks, excursions | Families can cover travel but not a cruise fare |
| Host only | The celebration dinner or event | Everything else | Everyone can afford their own trip |

Put whatever you agree in a short group email so nobody is guessing.

> **Quick math:** a couple covering four cabins for their children and partners on a 7-night Caribbean sailing, at about US$1,000 per person plus roughly US$150 each in taxes and fees, would spend around US$9,200 (roughly C$12,500–13,000). Prepaid gratuities for eight adults at about US$17 per day add close to US$950. Grandchildren sharing as third and fourth guests in a cabin usually cost far less than a full fare.

## Booking it as a group
Most lines treat about eight or more cabins on the same sailing as a group. Groups can bring a fixed rate, flexible deposits and sometimes an extra perk, such as onboard credit or a cocktail party. [How the eight-cabin group rule works](/articles/group-cruise-8-cabin-rule/) explains it.

### Choosing cabins
- Connecting cabins suit families with young children and their grandparents nearby.
- Keep everyone on the same deck if you can, so meeting up is easy.
- Book the anniversary couple a balcony; it becomes the family's gathering spot.

[Multigenerational cruise planning](/articles/multigenerational-cruise-planning-cabins/) covers cabin layouts in more detail.

## Choosing an itinerary for every age
The best sailings are easy to reach from where everyone lives. Alaska from Vancouver suits Western Canadian families in July or August, with no long flights for grandparents. Caribbean sailings from Florida suit Ontario and Quebec families during March break or summer.

Ships with a good kids' club, a calm adults-only area and flexible dining help keep three generations happy.

## Planning the celebration on board
Ask the line about dining together at one large table; [seating a big group together](/articles/group-dining-cruises-20-people/) covers how. A private dinner or cocktail reception can often be booked through the group or celebrations department. Some couples add a short vow renewal with the family watching; see [renewing your vows at sea](/articles/renewing-vows-at-sea/).

Leave plenty of unstructured time too. Most families remember the long breakfasts and port days together as much as the big dinner.

## A simple planning timeline
1. **12–18 months out:** agree on who pays, choose dates outside each family's busiest periods, and pick a ship.
2. **12 months out:** reserve the cabin block and collect deposits.
3. **9 months out:** confirm names, passports and any connecting cabin requests.
4. **About 3–4 months out:** final payment is usually due; check your line's date. Everyone should have travel insurance by now.
5. **2 months out:** book the celebration dinner, photos and any group excursion.
6. **On board:** confirm dinner details with the maître d' on day one.

Group deadlines can differ from individual bookings; see [group cruise deposits and deadlines](/articles/group-cruise-deadlines-deposits/).

## Surprise or no surprise?
A surprise is lovely in theory but hard with passports, flights and time off work to arrange. Most families involve the couple from the start, then keep one moment secret, such as a slideshow, letters from the grandchildren or a cake at dinner.

## If someone has to drop out
With a dozen or more people booked, someone may need to cancel. Each family should have its own insurance, and the organiser should know the cancellation terms. [What happens if someone drops out of a group cruise](/articles/what-if-someone-drops-out-group-cruise/) explains the options.
