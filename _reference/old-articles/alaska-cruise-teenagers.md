Teenagers rarely get excited about glaciers in advance. They do get excited about ziplines, bikes and helicopters, and about having some freedom on board. Build the week around those, and Alaska often becomes the trip they talk about most.

## Give them one big adventure day
One genuinely adrenaline-heavy excursion does more for teen buy-in than a week of scenic cruising. Let your teen choose it from a shortlist you can afford. Owning the decision turns "family holiday" into "my trip".

Here are typical picks by port. Prices are rough, per person at recent rates, and minimum ages and weights vary by operator.

## Check the ship's teen club before you book
Teen spaces range from a full lounge with gaming setups, a dance floor and late events to a corner of a shared room. Age bands differ too: some lines split younger and older teens, which matters if your 13-year-old and 17-year-old would rather not share. [Cruise ship arcades and video games](/articles/cruise-ship-arcade-video-games-teens/) covers what costs extra.

Some lines set curfews for unaccompanied under-18s late at night. Read your line's youth policy so the rules come from the cruise line, not from you.

> **Watch out:** most lines restrict cabins so that a guest under 21 must share with, or be booked alongside, an adult. If you want teens in their own cabin, ask about connecting or adjacent cabins booked together; see [connecting cabins vs one big cabin](/articles/connecting-cabins-one-big-cabin-families/).

## Wifi and phones
Most major lines now use satellite internet that is far better than it was a few years ago, so a teen can message friends and often stream. It costs extra per device on most fares, and coverage can drop in narrow fjords.

> **Canadian tip:** in Alaskan ports, phones connect to US networks, so a US roaming add-on from your Canadian carrier works there. At sea, switch to airplane mode, because the ship's maritime network can bill heavily. Details are in [cruise wifi and roaming for Canadians](/articles/cruise-wifi-phone-roaming-canadians/).

## Making the scenic days work
Glacier and Inside Passage days are long stretches of watching. A few things help:

1. Hand your teen the good camera or the binoculars and make them the family's wildlife spotter.
2. Set a photo challenge: whale tail, eagle, calving ice, a totem pole.
3. Agree on a time to be on deck for the glacier itself, then let them go.
4. Plan the pool, sports court or teen club for the hours in between.

If they take to the camera, [Alaska cruise photography from deck](/articles/best-alaska-cruise-photography/) has settings worth sharing.

## Timing for Canadian families
With teens, you are almost always sailing in July or August, when school is out across Canada. Those weeks are the busiest and dearest of the season, and the adventure excursions sell out first. Book the one big excursion as soon as your line's booking window opens.

## Building the teen-friendly week
Pick the ship for its teen club, the excursion with your teen, and leave plenty of unscheduled time. For younger siblings in the same family, [what ages work in Alaska](/articles/alaska-cruise-with-kids-ages/) helps balance the plan.
