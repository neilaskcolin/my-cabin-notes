Take all the photos you like of the ship, the scenery and your own group. The etiquette questions come up when other people are in the frame, and the rule of thumb is simple: if they'd notice, ask.

## Photographing crew
Crew members are photographed all day, and most don't mind at all. Your cabin steward, a favourite bartender or the waiter who remembered your coffee order will often be glad to pose for a farewell photo.

A few courtesies make it easy:
1. **Ask first** for any posed or close-up shot.
2. **Pick your moment.** Not while they're carrying trays or rushing between cabins.
3. **Respect a no.** Some crew prefer not to appear online, and that's their call.
4. **Stay out of crew areas.** Doors marked "crew only" are off limits, camera or not.

Galley and behind-the-scenes tours, where offered, normally welcome photos. Ship photographers are a different case: you can always decline their portrait, as covered in [whether cruise photo packages are worth it](/articles/cruise-photography-packages-worth-it/).

## Photographing other passengers

### Fine without asking
Wide shots of the pool deck, the atrium or a sail-away crowd, where nobody is the subject.

### Ask first, or skip it
Close-ups of strangers, people in swimwear, anyone dining, and other people's children. If you want to share a group shot online, crop or blur faces you don't know.

> **Good to know:** a quick "do you mind?" fixes almost every awkward moment. Offering to take a photo for another couple often gets you one in return.

## Where cameras usually aren't welcome
- **Spa, thermal suites, gym changing rooms and saunas**
- **Kids clubs**, where parents generally can't photograph other children
- **Casino tables**, where many ships restrict photography
- **Production shows**, which often ban video and flash
- **Medical centre and crew areas**

## Glacier days and scenic moments
When the ship reaches a glacier or sails under the Lions Gate Bridge, everyone heads for the same rail. Take your shots, then step back so others can step in. Leave big tripods in the cabin and keep selfie sticks low. A balcony is the only place you can linger as long as you like (see [where to watch glaciers from the ship](/articles/best-spot-cruise-ship-glacier-viewing/)).

## In port
Ask before photographing people in markets, at work or at home. Costumed performers and people posing with animals may expect a tip. At cultural performances in Alaska, such as Tlingit dance presentations, follow any photography guidance from the hosts; [Tlingit culture in Alaska ports](/articles/tlingit-culture-totem-poles-alaska-ports/) gives background.

Planning aerial footage? Read [drone rules for cruises](/articles/cruise-ship-drone-photography-rules/) first. For camera tips, see [Alaska cruise photography](/articles/best-alaska-cruise-photography/).

## Documenting your trip thoughtfully
Ask before close-ups, share the best spots, and keep cameras out of private spaces. Your photos will be just as good, and nobody else's cruise gets worse because of them.
