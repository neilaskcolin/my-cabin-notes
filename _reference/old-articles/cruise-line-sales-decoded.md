Most cruise sale emails are honest, but the biggest number in them is rarely the one that reaches your credit card. Here is how the common promotions translate into real dollars, and a five-minute test you can run on any offer.

## What does "up to 75% off the second guest" really mean?
The discount applies to one fare out of two, and only on the sailings where the line has chosen to go that deep. Take a cabin priced at US$1,500 per person, so US$3,000 for two before the promotion.

At 75% off the second guest, the second fare drops to US$375 and the cabin fare totals US$1,875. That is 37.5% off the fare, which is good, but half the headline. Now add about US$400 of taxes and port fees and roughly US$250 of gratuities for the week, none of which are discounted, and the saving on the trip falls to around 30%.

> **Quick math:** halve the headline. "75% off the second guest" is at most 37.5% off the cabin fare, "50% off the second guest" is 25%, and "60% off" is 30%.

The words "up to" matter too. The deepest rate is usually reserved for a handful of sailings or categories, while most departures in the same sale carry 30–50% off the second guest.

## The "was" price problem
A percentage off only means something if people actually paid the original price. Cruise fares move constantly (see [how cruise pricing works](/articles/how-cruise-pricing-works/)), so a "was" price can reflect a short-lived peak rather than what the cabin normally sells for. The arithmetic is accurate, but it tells you little about whether today's price is low.

The fix is to ignore the "was" price and compare the current total with what the same cabin costs on a neighbouring week or a similar ship.

## Common promotions, translated

| Promotion wording | What it usually means | Real value check |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 75% off 2nd guest | Second fare discounted, deepest rate on select sailings | Roughly 15–37% off the cabin fare |
| Kids sail free | Third and fourth guest fares waived on select dates | Taxes, port fees and gratuities still billed per child |
| Free drinks, wifi or tips bundle | Perks included, sometimes with a higher base fare | Worth it only if you would buy them; service charges may apply |
| US$200 onboard credit | Fixed spending money on your account | Close to face value if you will spend it |
| Reduced deposit | Smaller payment upfront | Helps cash flow; the fare is unchanged |
| Free upgrade | Booked one category higher | Compare with simply booking that category |
| Book by midnight | The current promotion ends | A new one usually replaces it within days |

The "free" child is a classic example: the fare disappears, but the per-person [taxes and port fees](/articles/taxes-port-fees-cruise-fare-breakdown/) and daily gratuities do not. The full breakdown is in [kids sail free promotions: the catch](/articles/kids-sail-free-catch/).

## Which promotions tend to save real money?
Fixed-dollar extras are the easiest to value. Onboard credit and prepaid gratuities either appear on your account or they don't, and most travellers spend that money anyway. Prepaid gratuities on a 7-night sailing are worth roughly US$220–300 per couple at recent rates, or about C$300–420.

Bundles with drinks and wifi can be excellent for heavy users and close to worthless for light ones. If you are weighing a credit against a price cut, [onboard credit vs a straight cash discount](/articles/cruise-onboard-credit-vs-cash-discount/) walks through the choice.

> **Watch out:** a bundle that adds drinks and wifi sometimes raises the base fare at the same time. Ask for the price of the same cabin without the bundle before deciding it is a deal.

## How much should urgency matter?
Countdown timers and "ends tonight" banners are standard marketing. Promotions rotate weekly, and the biggest pushes cluster around [wave season from January to March](/articles/wave-season-explained-canada/) and [Black Friday](/articles/black-friday-cruise-deals-real/). Missing one deadline rarely means missing the price.

If you book on a refundable fare, a better promotion that appears later can often be applied to your booking before final payment. That is the logic behind [asking for price protection](/articles/cruise-price-protection-asking-for-it/).

## A five-minute test for any cruise offer
1. Note the ship, sailing date and exact cabin category code, not just "balcony".
2. Add up fare, taxes and fees, and gratuities, in one currency. For a USD price, convert at your card's real rate.
3. Subtract the cash value of any extras you would genuinely use.
4. Compare that total with the same category on a nearby sailing, another line, or a quote from a few weeks ago.
5. Check the fare type, since a non-refundable deposit fare is cheaper partly because it gives you less flexibility.

## The short version
Halve any "off the second guest" headline, ignore "was" prices, and value extras at what you would really spend. For Canadians, run the comparison in Canadian dollars, because a USD sale price can look different once your card converts it.
