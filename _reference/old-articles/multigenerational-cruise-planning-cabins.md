Three generations on one ship works best when everyone has their own space and only some of the schedule is shared. Book neighbouring standard cabins rather than one big suite, meet for a few planned meals rather than every one, and keep each household's money separate within one group booking.

## Which cabin setup works for three generations?

| Setup | Sleeps | Bathrooms | Best for | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two connecting cabins | 4–8 | 2 | Grandparents next door to young grandkids | Connecting pairs sell out early |
| Cabins across the hall | 4–8 | 2 | Families with school-age or older kids | No inside door |
| Family suite | 4–6+ | 1–2 | Families who want one living space | Higher cost per bed, less privacy |
| Grandkids bunk with grandparents | 4–6 | 2 | Giving parents a genuine break | Grandparents take the night shift |

Two standard balconies often cost less than one suite with a similar number of beds, and they come with twice the bathrooms. [Suite vs two balcony cabins](/articles/suite-vs-two-balcony-cabins/) runs the numbers. Book every cabin in one group block so the family counts as a group for dining and perks.

### Placing the grandparents
Mid-ship cabins near an elevator mean shorter walks and less motion. If anyone uses a wheelchair or scooter, book an accessible cabin first, because there are few on each ship; [accessible cruising](/articles/accessible-cruising-mobility-ships/) covers what to ask. And keep nappers away from cabins under the pool deck or kids' splash area.

## Dining when bedtimes don't match
Grandparents and toddlers often want dinner at 5:30; teens and parents would rather eat at 8. Forcing one sitting every night is where many of these trips go sideways.

### What works instead
Book early fixed seating for the whole family on two or three planned nights, such as the first night, formal night and the last night. On other nights, early eaters use the buffet or early sitting while parents book a later table. Many kids' clubs run supervised dinners in the evening, which frees parents for a grown-up meal; see [kids' clubs: ages, hours and costs](/articles/kids-clubs-cruise-ships-ages-costs/).

Breakfast or lunch is often the easier daily get-together. Everyone is awake, nobody is overtired, and the main dining room is quieter.

## A sea day that works for every age
1. **8:00:** breakfast together at the buffet.
2. **9:30:** kids to the kids' club; grandparents to a talk or trivia; parents to the gym or pool.
3. **12:30:** lunch together in the main dining room.
4. **2:00:** grandparents take the grandkids to the pool while parents get two hours alone.
5. **5:30:** early dinner for grandparents and little ones.
6. **8:00:** parents and teens at a later dinner or the evening show.

On port days, choose one outing every generation can manage, such as a boat tour or a scenic train, and check walking levels before booking. Tender ports can be hard for anyone unsteady on steps.

## Budgets across generations
The cleanest structure is one group booking, separate payments: each household pays its own fare and onboard account. Agree on any shared costs, like a private excursion, before sailing.

When grandparents are treating the whole family, decide upfront exactly what is covered. Fares and gratuities are one thing; drink packages, specialty dinners and excursions add up fast, and a clear line avoids awkward moments at the end of the week.

> **Good to know:** gratuities are normally charged per person per day, children included. At about US$16–23 each, a family of four on a 7-night sailing adds roughly US$450–650, or about C$620–900, to the bill.

## Paperwork and insurance for each generation
Each child needs their own valid travel document, and a Canadian passport is the simplest; [passports, NEXUS and ID](/articles/passports-nexus-id-cruise-documents/) explains the options. If grandchildren sail with grandparents or with only one parent, carry a signed consent letter from the absent parent or parents, which the Government of Canada recommends.

Provincial health plans pay little or nothing at sea. Grandparents in their 60s and 70s usually face medical questionnaires and higher premiums, so they should shop early and declare conditions accurately; see [cruise insurance for seniors in Canada](/articles/cruise-insurance-seniors-canada-pre-existing/).

> **Example:** grandparents from Winnipeg, two adult children and four grandkids book three neighbouring balconies on a 7-night Alaska sailing from Vancouver in July. They eat together three nights, the kids' club covers two evenings, and each household signs for its own drinks.

## Plan for space, not constant togetherness
The best multigenerational trips give everyone room to breathe and a few moments that bring the whole family together.
