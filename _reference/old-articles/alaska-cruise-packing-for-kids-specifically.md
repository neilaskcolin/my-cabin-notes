Kids feel the cold faster than adults, get wetter, and lose mittens at a remarkable rate. Pack them the same layering system you use, then add the extras below. Canadian families already own most of it; you rarely need to buy anything new.

## The core layers, kid-sized
Start with the system from [what to pack for May vs September](/articles/alaska-packing-may-vs-september/), scaled down:

- **Base layer:** two sets of synthetic or merino long underwear. Cotton stays damp.
- **Mid layer:** a fleece or light puffy. A fall jacket from home usually works.
- **Shell:** a truly waterproof jacket with a hood that fits over a toque.
- **Rain pants:** the item parents most often forget. Kids sit on wet benches and kneel on docks.
- **Toque, neck warmer and waterproof mittens:** pack a spare set of each.

Choose big zippers and simple fastenings so a six-year-old can add or shed a layer without help. That matters on glacier days, when the deck swings from sunny to freezing.

## Footwear and socks
Waterproof hikers or lined rain boots, already broken in, plus a pair of runners for the ship. Pack more socks than you think: at least one spare pair in your day bag for every port. Wet feet end a kid's port day faster than anything else, and rain is likely somewhere on almost every sailing (see [your first Alaska cruise](/articles/first-alaska-cruise-what-to-know/)).

> **Watch out:** most excursion operators have minimum ages and sometimes height or weight limits for ziplines, helicopters and kayaks. Check before you build a day around one, and see [what ages actually work in Alaska](/articles/alaska-cruise-with-kids-ages/) for realistic picks.

## Things kids need that adults don't
### For the ship
- A swimsuit and quick-dry towel wrap. Many ships have heated or covered pools and hot tubs, and kids use them even in Alaska.
- A night light and a small reading lamp if you are sharing a dark inside cabin.
- Familiar snacks for picky eaters, especially on travel days.
- A refillable water bottle each.

### For wildlife and glacier days
- Kid-sized binoculars. Adults rarely share theirs when a whale surfaces.
- A cheap waterproof camera or an old phone in airplane mode, so they can take their own photos.
- A notebook for a wildlife checklist: eagle, humpback, sea lion, bear.
- In Glacier Bay, park rangers often run Junior Ranger activities on board, so pack pencils.

### For the quiet stretches
Scenic cruising involves long spells of watching. Download shows, audiobooks and games at home, since ship wifi costs extra. A deck of cards and a travel game help in the dining room.

## Health and comfort
Pack children's sunscreen and sunglasses, because glare off ice and water is strong. Bring lip balm, a basic first-aid kit and any regular medicines in their original containers. For motion, children's dimenhydrinate is sold in Canadian pharmacies, but check doses with a pharmacist; [seasickness in kids](/articles/seasickness-in-kids-what-to-do/) covers what else helps.

> **Example:** a family of four from Winnipeg flying to Vancouver can fit everything into two checked bags and four carry-ons by using one packing cube per child and wearing the bulkiest jackets on the plane. Ship laundry handles the mid-week refresh.

## Documents for the kids
Carry each child's passport, health card and any custody or consent papers in one waterproof pouch. Provincial health plans cover little outside Canada, so check that your travel insurance names every child. ## What to leave at home
Skip snow pants and winter boots, which are overkill in summer, and umbrellas, which the deck wind destroys. Leave bulky toys; kids clubs supply plenty. If you are bringing a toddler, [strollers and car seats on a cruise](/articles/bringing-stroller-car-seat-cruise/) covers what is worth hauling.
