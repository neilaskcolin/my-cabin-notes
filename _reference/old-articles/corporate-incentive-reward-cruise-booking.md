An incentive cruise is a group booking with a corporate twist: you have to hold the cabins before you know who's earned them. Get the timeline, the qualification rules and the tax treatment right, and a cruise makes a reward people will chase all year.

## How an incentive cruise is booked
Most incentive trips are a standard group block, booked through the cruise line's group or corporate sales team or through a licensed agency. The company pays centrally, rather than each employee paying their own way. Group status usually starts at about eight cabins, as explained in [how group cruise bookings work](/articles/group-cruise-8-cabin-rule/).

Larger programmes negotiate a written contract with deposit dates, release dates and amenities set out. The very largest companies charter a whole ship, which gives full control of the itinerary and the public spaces.

## The incentive cruise timeline
1. **18–12 months out:** set the budget, decide who's eligible and whether partners are included, and choose a sailing.
2. **12 months out:** hold the cabin block. Negotiate a release date that falls *after* the qualifying period ends, so unused cabins go back without penalty.
3. **Qualifying period:** often 6–12 months of sales or performance results. Share progress regularly so people stay motivated.
4. **Results announced:** winners confirm, and the company releases the cabins it doesn't need.
5. **Names deadline:** submit passenger names exactly as they appear on passports.
6. **Final payment:** usually several months before sailing for groups. Check your contract.
7. **Sail:** with welcome gifts, a recognition dinner and staff on hand.

## Writing qualification rules people trust
- Base them on measurable results, like sales targets, customer scores or project milestones, not manager discretion.
- Publish the rules at the start, in writing, with the exact qualifying dates.
- Decide what happens to part-timers, mid-year hires and people on leave.
- Set the number of places, or a threshold anyone can hit. Both work, but people should know which one applies.
- Say upfront whether the trip can be swapped for cash. Many companies don't allow it.

## Who pays for what
### What the company usually covers
The cruise fare, taxes and port fees, gratuities, and flights from each winner's home city. For a national Canadian team, that might mean staff from Halifax, Montreal and Vancouver converging on Fort Lauderdale, or on Vancouver for an Alaska sailing. Many programmes add onboard credit, or a drink package for a premium feel.

### What winners usually cover
Optional extras like spa treatments, extra excursions, casino spending, and any pre- or post-cruise nights they add themselves. Spell this out in the welcome pack to avoid awkward bills at the end of the week.

### Partners
Including a partner roughly doubles the cost per winner. Some companies cover the winner in full and offer partners the group rate at their own expense, which is a middle ground worth considering.

## Making it feel like a reward
Plan a private welcome reception on the first sea day, a recognition dinner with a few words from leadership, and small branded gifts in each cabin. Groups of this size can usually reserve private event space, which is covered in [what group organisers get free](/articles/group-cruise-perks-organiser/). A photographer for the recognition dinner gives people something to keep, as discussed in [hiring a photographer for a group cruise](/articles/hiring-professional-photographer-group-cruise/).

## Tax and payroll
> **Canadian tip:** in Canada, an employer-paid reward trip is generally a taxable benefit to the employee, and its value may need to be reported on their T4. Some companies "gross up" the benefit so winners aren't out of pocket. Confirm the treatment with your accountant before announcing the trip, so the reward doesn't arrive with a surprise tax bill.
