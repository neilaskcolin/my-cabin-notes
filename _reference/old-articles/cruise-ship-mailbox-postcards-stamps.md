Yes, you can send postcards from a cruise, but there's no ship's postbox that empties every morning. Your card has to enter a national postal system somewhere, and the stamp has to match the country where that happens.

## Which stamps do you need?
This is where most people go wrong. A stamp is valid only in the country that issued it, so the stamp depends on where the card is posted, not where you bought it or where you live.

| Where the card is posted | Stamps to use | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Vancouver, before boarding | Canadian | Use a downtown postbox before you board |
| Alaska ports | US international postcard stamps | Post offices are close to the docks in the main ports |
| US Virgin Islands, Puerto Rico | US stamps | Same rates as the mainland |
| Mexico and most Caribbean islands | That country's stamps | Delivery is slow and less predictable |
| Europe | That country's stamps | Often sold at tobacconists and newsagents |
| Handed to guest services | Ask which port it'll go from | Buy stamps for that country |

A US international postcard stamp is about US$1.70 at recent rates; confirm at the counter.

## How to send a postcard from your cruise
1. **Buy cards early.** The gift shop sells ship postcards; ports sell scenic ones for less.
2. **Ask guest services on day one** whether they collect mail, which port it goes from and whether they sell stamps.
3. **Write them on a sea day** so they're ready before the first port.
4. **Post them yourself at the first suitable port** if you can. A post office drop is more reliable than mail passed through the ship.
5. **Write "Canada" clearly on the last line** of the address, in capitals.

## Why postcards arrive after you do
Mail given to the ship sits until a port with a reliable postal link, then joins that country's international mail. Cards posted in Alaska usually take one to three weeks to reach Canada. From some Caribbean and Mexican ports, several weeks is normal, and some cards never turn up.

> **Canadian tip:** on a round trip from Vancouver, write your cards on the final sea day and post them with Canadian stamps after you disembark. It's often the fastest route home, though the cards no longer carry an Alaska postmark.

## Is it still worth it?
For grandparents, classrooms and kids who collect stamps, yes. A card from Ketchikan or Cozumel with a foreign stamp and postmark is a small souvenir that a photo message can't match. Treat it as a keepsake, not news.

If speed matters more than postmarks, postcard apps can print your own photo and mail it from a printer in Canada, which needs only a few minutes of wifi (see [how cruise ship internet works](/articles/cruise-ship-internet-how-it-works/)).

## Continuing this classic travel tradition
Sort the stamps for each country, post cards ashore whenever you can, and tell the recipients they're coming. The postcard is the souvenir; the delay is part of the charm.
