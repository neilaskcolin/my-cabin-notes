You can almost always get cruise insurance as a Canadian senior, even with health conditions. What decides whether it actually pays is how honestly you fill in the medical questionnaire, whether your conditions meet the stability rules, and whether the policy covers the whole trip.

## Why medical cover matters more than cancellation
A ship's medical centre is a private clinic that bills your onboard account, and your provincial health plan doesn't cover it. Once you are ashore in Alaska, Florida or the Caribbean, you are paying US or local hospital rates. A helicopter or air ambulance evacuation from a remote fjord or a Caribbean sea day can exceed US$100,000. [Cruise medical care costs](/articles/cruise-medical-care-costs/) explains what's on board and what it charges.

Provincial plans pay only a small fraction of out-of-country emergency costs. That is why emergency medical cover of $5 million or more is the baseline, before you even think about cancellation.

## How age changes your policy
Premiums rise in steps, commonly at 60, 65, 70 and 75, and again past 80. Many insurers add a medical questionnaire at 60 or 65, and the answers decide your rate and what's covered.

### The questionnaire is where claims are won or lost
Answer every question exactly, using your medication list and doctor's records rather than memory. An incorrect answer, even an innocent one, is the most common reason claims are refused. If you're unsure whether something counts, phone the insurer and note the name and date of the call.

A few insurers cap cover at 80 or 85. Others go higher at a price, so a couple of different ages may end up with different insurers.

## The stability clause, in plain English
Insurers cover a pre-existing condition only if it has been stable for a set period before departure, typically 90, 120 or 180 days. Stable usually means:

- no new symptoms or worsening of existing ones
- no new medication, and no change in dose or frequency
- no new tests, referrals to a specialist, or results you're waiting for
- no hospital stay or emergency visit for that condition

Many policies treat a switch to a generic drug at the same dose, or routine insulin adjustments, as not a change. Read the definitions page, because the wording varies between insurers.

> **Example:** a Winnipeg couple book a 14-night cruise. Ten weeks before sailing, his blood-pressure dose is increased. On a 90-day stability policy, his blood pressure and related heart conditions may now be excluded. The fix is to call the insurer before travel, since some will re-underwrite for an extra premium or offer a plan with a shorter stability period.

## Trip-length limits
Standard senior policies often cover 30 or 60 days per trip. A 14-night cruise plus pre- and post-cruise hotel nights fits easily, but back-to-back sailings, a long repositioning or a winter in Florida followed by a cruise may need an extension. Plan it before you go, as covered in [longer cruises for retirees](/articles/longer-cruises-retirees-14-nights/).

> **Watch out:** if your condition changes after you buy but before you leave, tell the insurer. Cover is usually assessed against your health on the departure date, not the purchase date.

## When to buy, step by step
1. **At deposit time,** buy cancellation and interruption cover. If a condition worsens before sailing and you must cancel, you're only covered if the policy predates the change. Some benefits only apply if you buy within a short window after the deposit.
2. **Check your card and workplace plans** before paying for duplicate cover (see below).
3. **Complete the medical questionnaire** with your medication list in front of you.
4. **Confirm the stability period** and any exclusions in writing.
5. **Recheck 90–180 days before departure** whether anything has changed, and call the insurer if so.
6. **Carry the policy number and emergency line** on paper and on your phone.

[Cruise insurance in Canada](/articles/travel-insurance-cruise-canadians/) covers general policy types, and [cancel-for-any-reason cover](/articles/cancel-for-any-reason-cruise-insurance-explained/) explains the add-on for extra flexibility.

## Credit-card, workplace and cruise-line plans
Many card plans reduce or drop medical cover from 65 and cap trip length at around 15 days or less. Retiree workplace plans can be generous but may require you to call before seeking treatment. Cruise-line protection plans often refund cancellations as future cruise credit and carry limited medical cover, compared in [travel insurance vs cruise line protection](/articles/travel-insurance-vs-cruise-protection/).

### Questions to ask before you buy
- Is there a maximum age, and does it apply on the purchase or departure date?
- What is the stability period for each of my conditions?
- Does the policy cover medical evacuation from a ship, and repatriation home?
- Are missed ports, itinerary changes and missed embarkation covered?
- What is the maximum trip length, and can I extend from abroad?

## Getting the policy matched to the cruise
Buy early, declare everything and keep notes of every call. A licensed travel insurance advisor or cruise consultant who regularly books Canadians over 60 can point you to insurers that handle age bands and stability clauses sensibly.
