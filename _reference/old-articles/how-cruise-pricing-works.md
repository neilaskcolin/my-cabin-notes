The fare you are refreshing is not a fixed price. It is a live number that moves with everyone else's bookings, the fare type you choose and the number of people in the cabin. Once you see the moving parts, the swings stop feeling random.

## How do cruise lines set prices?
Each sailing is split into dozens of cabin categories by type, deck and location. Every category has a sales target for each point in the run-up to departure. When a category sells ahead of target, its price climbs. When it lags, the line adds a promotion, such as onboard credit or a drinks offer, or quietly cuts the fare.

Group space held back for agencies and organisers gets released into general sale at set points, which can briefly add inventory. Multiply that by every ship and departure, and you get what people see: identical cabins, different weeks, prices hundreds of dollars apart.

## Why does the same cabin have three prices?
At any moment, the same balcony on the same sailing is often sold several ways. The figures below are illustrative, per person:

| Fare type | Example price | What is different |
|---|---|---|
| Non-refundable deposit fare | US$1,350 | Cheapest, but the deposit is lost or turned into credit if you cancel |
| Standard refundable fare | US$1,450 | Deposit refundable until final payment; easiest to reprice |
| Bundle fare with drinks and wifi | US$1,750 | Extras included; worth it only if you would buy them |

On top of fare type, the price changes with who is booking:

- **Occupancy.** Prices assume two people. Solos pay a supplement, and third and fourth guests usually pay less.
- **Eligibility rates.** Seniors, residents of certain provinces or states, military and first responders, and past guests sometimes see lower fares (see [military, first responder and senior discounts](/articles/military-first-responder-senior-discounts-cruise/)).
- **Cabin assignment.** A guarantee cabin, where the line picks your exact room, is often cheaper than choosing one (see [guarantee cabins](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/)).
- **Currency.** Canadians are often shown a CAD fare based on the line's internal exchange rate, which may be slightly better or worse than the USD fare converted by your card.

> **Quick math:** "US$1,200 per person, double occupancy" means US$2,400 for the cabin. A solo traveller paying a 200% supplement pays the full US$2,400 alone; at 150% it is US$1,800. [The single supplement explained](/articles/single-supplement-how-to-dodge/) covers ways around it.

## The predictable swings inside the noise
Daily movement is noisy, but some patterns repeat each year:

- **Launch pricing.** Itineraries open one to two years out, and fares on popular sailings tend to rise from there.
- **Wave season, January to March.** The year's biggest promotional push (see [wave season explained](/articles/wave-season-explained-canada/)).
- **Shoulder discounting.** Edge-of-season sailings, such as May and September in Alaska, get extra help.
- **The final payment cliff.** Around 75–120 days out, cancellations return cabins. Soft sailings get cheaper; popular ones keep climbing.
- **Rotating extras.** Promotions change weekly even when the headline fare holds still.

Taxes and port fees are set separately and are not part of these swings, as explained in [why your cruise costs more than the advertised price](/articles/taxes-port-fees-cruise-fare-breakdown/). Once you have booked, the line generally cannot raise your fare, covered in [can a cruise line raise your fare after booking](/articles/can-cruise-line-raise-fare-after-booking/).

## What this means for buyers
Trying to time the absolute bottom rarely works, because the line has more data and more patience. The strategy that beats the system doesn't need a prediction:

- Book early on a refundable fare, locking in your cabin choice and today's promotion.
- Watch the sailing until final payment, using [price alerts](/articles/how-to-set-cruise-price-alerts/) or a weekly check.
- Ask for a reprice whenever the fare drops (see [cruise price protection](/articles/cruise-price-protection-asking-for-it/)).

That way price volatility works for you: increases don't touch you once you've booked, and decreases can be claimed.

## Who should consider a non-refundable fare?
Travellers with fixed dates, solid insurance and no doubt about going can save a little with a non-refundable deposit. Anyone with health, work or family uncertainty usually does better paying slightly more for flexibility. [Cruise fare classes explained](/articles/cruise-fare-classes-refundable/) sets out the trade-off.

## Reading any fare in ten seconds
Ask three questions: which fare type is this, for how many people, and in which currency? Answer those and two prices that looked wildly different often turn out to be the same cabin sold two ways.
