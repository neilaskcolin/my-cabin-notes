faq1q: How much cheaper are non-refundable cruise fares?
faq1a: Typically a modest saving, often around US$50–200 per person, against a deposit of similar or larger size that you lose if you cancel. The discount rarely makes up for genuinely uncertain plans.
faq2q: Can you change ships or dates on a non-refundable fare?
faq2a: Usually yes, for a per-person change fee plus any fare difference, and some versions convert the deposit to future cruise credit instead of losing it. Read the specific fare rules before booking.
faq3q: Is there any difference between the two fares after final payment?
faq3a: Very little. Once final payment is made, both fare types fall under the same cancellation penalty schedule. The difference only applies in the months between deposit and final payment.

At checkout you will often see two prices for the same cabin. The cheaper one has a non-refundable deposit; the dearer one lets you cancel for free until final payment. The real question the cheaper fare asks is how sure you are that nothing will change for the next several months.

## How the two fare types compare
| | Refundable (standard) fare | Non-refundable deposit fare |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Higher | Lower, often by US$50–200 per person |
| Cancel before final payment | Deposit refunded in full | Deposit kept, sometimes returned as future cruise credit minus a fee |
| Change ship or date | Usually free before final payment, at the current fare | Change fee per person plus any fare difference |
| Moving onto a lower price | Usually straightforward | May count as a change and attract a fee |
| Name changes | Often allowed | Often restricted |
| After final payment | Normal cancellation penalties | Same penalties |

Names vary by line. Royal Caribbean and Celebrity, for example, label theirs "non-refundable deposit" fares, while Carnival's Early Saver fare also comes with a non-refundable deposit. Read the fare rules on the booking page rather than relying on the name.

## Running the numbers
> **Quick math:** a couple saves US$100 each, US$200 in total, by choosing the non-refundable fare. Their deposit is US$400 each, so US$800 is at risk. If there is more than about a one-in-four chance that illness, work, family or money will make them cancel or change before final payment, the refundable fare is cheaper on average.

That calculation ignores another cost. A locked booking is harder to reprice, so if a better promotion appears, the change fee can eat the gain. A refundable fare stays a live option in a moving market. [Price drops after booking](/articles/price-drop-after-booking-cruise/) explains how that works.

## Who should choose each fare
Non-refundable makes sense for:
- Fixed-date trips such as a milestone anniversary or a wedding at sea.
- Families booking a school-holiday week they will not move.
- Last-minute bookings already inside the final payment window, where everything is non-refundable anyway.

Refundable makes sense for:
- Anyone booking a year or more ahead.
- Older travellers or anyone with a health condition that could flare up.
- People still comparing ships, or hoping to catch a better sale. See [booking 2027 now vs waiting](/articles/booking-2027-now-vs-waiting/).

For Canadians, travel insurance bought at the deposit stage can cover cancellation for listed reasons, such as serious illness, on either fare. It won't cover a change of heart. If you want that, a refundable fare is the cheapest version before final payment; after it, see [cancel-for-any-reason insurance](/articles/cancel-for-any-reason-cruise-insurance-explained/).

## The default that suits most people
Choose the refundable fare, book early, buy insurance at the deposit stage and keep watching the price until final payment. The small premium buys months of flexibility. For how those months play out, see [deposits and final payment dates](/articles/cruise-deposits-final-payment/).
