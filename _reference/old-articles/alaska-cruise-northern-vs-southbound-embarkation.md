The ship sees the same coastline whichever way it sails. What direction really decides is where your trip starts and ends, which matters a lot when one end is Vancouver and the other is a small Alaskan port a couple of hours from Anchorage.

## Does the scenery change with direction?
Mostly no. A typical one-way 7-night route between Vancouver and Seward or Whittier calls at Ketchikan, Juneau and Skagway or Icy Strait Point, then visits Hubbard Glacier or College Fjord. Ports may come in a different order or on different weekdays, so check both directions on your chosen ship. For the route itself, see [Inside Passage vs Gulf of Alaska](/articles/inside-passage-vs-gulf-of-alaska/).

The one difference people notice is pacing. Northbound saves the biggest glacier scenery for the last days. Southbound opens with it.

## What changes: a side-by-side
| | Northbound (Vancouver to Alaska) | Southbound (Alaska to Vancouver) |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Canada Place, easy to reach from anywhere in Canada | Seward or Whittier, after a flight to Anchorage and a transfer |
| Night before | Vancouver hotel | Anchorage hotel |
| Open-water Gulf crossing | Near the end of the cruise | In the first day or two |
| Big glacier day | Late in the trip | Early in the trip |
| Ship's clocks | Go back an hour to Alaska time | Go forward an hour to Pacific time |
| End | Transfer to Anchorage, then fly home | Walk off in Vancouver |
| Re-entry to Canada | CBSA at your home airport | CBSA at Canada Place, or at your airport if you connect |

## Flights are the real decider
Getting home from Anchorage to Calgary, Edmonton, Toronto or Montreal usually means a connection, often through Seattle or Vancouver, and nonstop options are limited and seasonal. Disembarkation day northbound also includes a one- to three-hour transfer from the port before you reach the airport. [Anchorage vs Whittier vs Seward](/articles/anchorage-vs-whittier-vs-seward/) explains those transfers.

Southbound flips that problem to the start, where it is easier to absorb: you can fly to Anchorage a day or two early and explore. At the end, you step off at Canada Place with the whole of Vancouver's flight network a SkyTrain ride away.

> **Canadian tip:** a southbound cruise ending in Vancouver pairs well with a train trip into the Rockies. The Rocky Mountaineer runs from Vancouver to Banff, Lake Louise or Jasper, and for Albertans it doubles as the journey home.

## How Denali fits each direction
On a cruisetour, direction decides whether land comes first or last:

1. **Northbound:** cruise first, then rail inland to Denali, then fly home from Anchorage or Fairbanks. The trip builds to a finale, but the last days are busy and you travel home tired.
2. **Southbound:** fly to Fairbanks or Anchorage, tour Denali, then board the ship. Land days are the busy part, and the cruise becomes the slow, restful finish.

One practical point for southbound: you board with several days of laundry, so pack enough for the land portion or plan on the ship's laundry service. More detail is in [Denali before or after the cruise](/articles/denali-add-on-before-after-cruise/).

## Sea legs and the Gulf
The Gulf of Alaska is the only properly exposed stretch on these routes. Southbound passengers usually meet it within the first day or two, before they have found their sea legs. Northbound passengers cross it near the end. If you are prone to motion sickness, read [Gulf of Alaska crossing seasickness](/articles/alaska-gulf-crossing-seasickness/) and pack remedies in your carry-on.

## So which should you choose?
Choose northbound if you want an easy start from Vancouver and a big-scenery finish, and you do not mind a longer journey home. Choose southbound if you want the simplest trip home to Canada, plan to add the Rockies, or prefer to get the busy land portion done first. Either way, price your flights in both directions before you pick the sailing.
