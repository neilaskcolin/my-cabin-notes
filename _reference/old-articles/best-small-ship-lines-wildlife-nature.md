If the wildlife is why you're going, ship size matters more than almost anything else. A small expedition-style ship can follow a humpback into a quiet bay, put you in a kayak beside a sea otter, or land you on a beach where bears dig for clams. A 3,000-passenger ship, however well run, can't.

## Why do small ships see more wildlife?
- **Access:** narrow inlets and shallow bays that big ships can't enter.
- **Flexibility:** captains can stop for whales or reroute to where wildlife is active.
- **Getting off the ship:** skiffs, Zodiacs and kayaks put you at water level.
- **Expert guides:** naturalists lead the outings and share what you're seeing.
- **Quiet:** fewer people and less noise, both on deck and ashore.

> **Good to know:** in Antarctica, industry rules generally limit landings to ships carrying no more than 500 passengers, and only 100 people may be ashore at a site at once. Large ships often can't land guests at all, which is why expedition ships dominate there.

### Alaska
Alaska is the easiest small-ship trip for Canadians, and the contrast with big ships is sharp. Small vessels spend days in places such as Frederick Sound or remote coves, with kayaking, hiking and skiff trips included. Alaskan Dream Cruises is Alaska Native-owned, and itineraries often include cultural visits. Our guide to [small ship vs big ship for Alaska](/articles/small-ship-vs-big-ship-alaska/) compares the two in detail, and [whale watching on an Alaska cruise](/articles/whale-watching-alaska-cruise-sailings/) shows where whales concentrate.

### What a small-ship day looks like
A typical day might start with a skiff ride along a shoreline at low tide, scanning for bears turning over rocks. After lunch, guests split between kayaking and a guided forest walk, while the ship repositions to a new bay. Before dinner, a naturalist recaps the day's sightings, and the captain may change tomorrow's plan if whales were spotted nearby. There are no ports with shops on many days, and that is the point.

### Further afield
The Galápagos is the most accessible true expedition cruise, with flights via Ecuador and wildlife that ignores you at arm's length (see the [Galápagos expedition cruise guide](/articles/galapagos-expedition-cruise-canadians/)). Antarctica is the big-ticket trip, weighed up in [Antarctica expedition cruise cost](/articles/antarctica-expedition-cruise-cost-worth-it/). The Canadian Arctic offers polar bears, walrus and Inuit communities without leaving the country for most of the trip.

> **Canadian tip:** for the Alaska small ships, fly into Juneau or Sitka a day early. Weather delays on regional flights are common, and small ships can't hold departure for late arrivals the way a port agent might arrange on a big line.

## The trade-offs
Small ships are not for everyone. Expect smaller cabins, fewer dining choices, little or no evening entertainment, and more motion in open water. Boarding skiffs or Zodiacs needs reasonable balance and mobility. Medical facilities are more limited than on a big ship, so check your travel insurance includes evacuation.

## Questions to ask before you book
1. How many guests does the ship carry, and how many naturalists?
2. Which activities are included: kayaks, skiffs, guided hikes, landings?
3. How much flexibility does the captain have to change the plan for wildlife?
4. What mobility is needed for landings and skiff transfers?
5. What's not included: flights, gratuities, drinks, hotel nights?
6. Where exactly does it start and end, and how do I get there from my city?

## Is a small ship worth it for you?
If you'd happily trade the theatre, casino and waterslides for a day spent following whales, a small ship is worth the premium. If wildlife is one interest among several, a big ship in Alaska with one or two good excursions, such as [bear viewing](/articles/bear-viewing-excursions-alaska/), delivers a lot for much less.
