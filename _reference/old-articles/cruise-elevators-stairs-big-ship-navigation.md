On a big ship, elevators are quick most of the day and painfully slow at a handful of predictable moments. Learn those peaks, know where every stairwell and elevator bank is, and moving between decks becomes easy.

## When are cruise ship elevators busiest?
- **Boarding afternoon,** when thousands of people head for cabins at once, many with carry-on bags.
- **Before dinner and shows,** especially the half hour before main dining seatings.
- **Sunset on sea days,** when the pool deck empties all at once.
- **Muster check-in,** before sail-away.
- **Disembarkation morning,** with everyone heading down to the exit decks on schedule.

Outside these windows, waits are usually short. [Your first day at sea](/articles/first-day-at-sea-what-to-expect/) covers how embarkation day flows.

## How elevators and stairs are laid out
Most big ships have three groups of elevators and stairs: forward, midship and aft. Each group usually has its own stairwell running alongside. Midship banks are busiest because they serve the main restaurants, atrium and pool deck. Forward and aft banks are often noticeably quieter.

Some ships skip deck 13, so don't be thrown when the buttons jump from 12 to 14. Deck plans on landings and in the cruise app show what's on each level. [How to find your way around a large ship](/articles/navigating-large-cruise-ship-without-getting-lost/) explains how to orient yourself in the first day.

> **Good to know:** stairwell landings often have a deck map and a "you are here" marker showing forward and aft. If you're lost, head to the nearest stairwell, not the nearest corridor.

### When to take the stairs
For two or three decks, especially going down, stairs usually win at peak times. It also adds some easy exercise to a week of generous eating. Going up seven decks after dinner is another matter.

## Tips for passengers with limited mobility
1. **Book a cabin near an elevator bank,** but not directly beside it if you're a light sleeper. Accessible cabins are often placed near elevators for this reason (see [accessible cruising on mobility-friendly ships](/articles/accessible-cruising-mobility-ships/)).
2. **Try the forward or aft bank** when midship is crowded.
3. **Leave a few minutes early** before dinner and shows.
4. **Ask crew for help at peak times.** On embarkation and disembarkation days, crew often manage queues and can help guests with wheelchairs or scooters.
5. **Tell guest services if you'd need help in an emergency,** so the crew has a record.
6. **Plan tender ports separately.** Elevators don't help with tender boarding, which can involve steps and a moving platform. [Cruise ship tenders explained](/articles/cruise-ship-tenders-explained/) covers what to expect.

## Elevator etiquette
Let people off before getting on, hold the door only briefly, and give priority to wheelchairs, scooters, strollers and anyone who can't manage stairs. If you're fit and travelling one or two decks at a busy time, taking the stairs frees up space for those who can't.

## Choose your cabin with movement in mind
If getting around matters to you, check where the elevator banks are on the deck plan before booking. A cabin a short walk from a quieter forward or aft bank is often the sweet spot. [Reading a cruise ship deck plan](/articles/reading-cruise-ship-deck-plans/) shows how to spot them.
