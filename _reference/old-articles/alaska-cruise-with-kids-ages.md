The best age for Alaska is when a child can stand at the rail for twenty minutes waiting for a whale and still be excited when it appears. For most kids, that starts at around 7. Younger children can still have a lovely cruise; they just enjoy the ship more than the destination.

## Under 3: you are buying the ship
Most lines accept infants from 6 months on a 7-night Alaska sailing. Some ships have paid nurseries for babies and toddlers, but free kids clubs usually start at 3. Glaciers mean little at this age, and long, cold scenic days work against nap schedules.

If you do go, a balcony cabin earns its price: you can watch the scenery during naps instead of being stuck inside. [Cruising with a baby or toddler](/articles/cruising-with-baby-toddler/) covers sleep, cribs and food.

## Ages 3 to 6: ship first, Alaska in small doses
Kids club opens up, along with pools, splash areas and character activities on some lines. Alaska works best in short bursts: a 20-minute look at a glacier, a tram ride, a short walk to see salmon. Plan one modest outing per port and let the afternoon be ship time.

### Good picks at this age
- The Mount Roberts Tramway in Juneau, starting right beside the docks.
- The paved trail at Mendenhall Glacier.
- A short wildlife boat trip on a larger, enclosed boat.
- Skagway's gold-rush town on foot, with a stop at the national park visitor centre.

## Ages 7 to 12: the sweet spot
This is the age Alaska was made for. Children are old enough for longer whale watches, sled-dog camps, salmon hatcheries and easy hikes, and young enough to be thrilled by a breaching humpback. In Glacier Bay, rangers often run Junior Ranger activities on board, and kids can earn a badge.

### Good picks at this age
- Whale watching in Juneau or Icy Strait Point.
- A sled-dog camp visit; summer camps use wheeled carts on the ground or sleds on a glacier, reached by helicopter.
- The White Pass railway from Skagway.
- A lumberjack show and totem park in Ketchikan.

> **Example:** a family from Calgary with kids aged 8 and 11 might do a whale watch in Juneau, the railway in Skagway and a free walk in Ketchikan. That keeps excursion spending to three paid tours while giving each child a highlight.

## Ages 13 to 17: give them the adventure
Teenagers come alive on ziplines, bikes and glacier trips, and most ships run a separate teen club. Let them pick one big excursion. The full plan is in [Alaska with teenagers](/articles/alaska-cruise-teenagers/).

## Family logistics that matter
- **Cabins:** third and fourth guests in one cabin are usually cheaper, but two cabins across the hall can be more comfortable and sometimes cost less than a family suite. Compare both, and see [connecting cabins vs one big cabin](/articles/connecting-cabins-one-big-cabin-families/).
- **Kids clubs:** age bands, hours and costs vary by line; [kids clubs on cruise ships](/articles/kids-clubs-cruise-ships-ages-costs/) compares them.
- **Clothing:** cold kids end scenic cruising early. [Packing for kids in Alaska](/articles/alaska-cruise-packing-for-kids-specifically/) has the checklist.
- **Excursion rules:** minimum ages, heights and weights vary by operator, so check them before promising a child a zipline.

> **Canadian tip:** school calendars push most families into July and August, the dearest weeks. If your province finishes in late June, the last week of June can be cheaper and has the longest days of the year. If one parent is travelling alone with the kids, carry a signed consent letter from the other parent.

## So which age is best?
If you can choose, wait until the youngest is around 7. If you can't, go anyway, but pick a ship with a strong kids club and keep the port days short. Children of every age remember the whales.
