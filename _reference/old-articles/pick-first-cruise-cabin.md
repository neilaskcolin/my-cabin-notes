First-timers tend to shop by category and price. Repeat cruisers shop by deck plan, then category. The second approach often costs the same and sleeps far better.

## Location first: the rules that matter most
- **Midship beats the ends.** The middle of the ship moves least in swell and is closest to everything. Forward cabins feel more motion; aft cabins can pick up engine vibration.
- **Middle decks beat the extremes.** High decks sway more in rough water; the lowest cabin decks are steady but can be near tender platforms or machinery.
- **Check above and below.** A cabin under the pool deck hears chairs scraping at 6 am. One over the theatre or a nightclub hears bass at midnight.
- **Mind the corridor.** Rooms beside elevator lobbies, crew service doors, laundry rooms and stairwells get the most foot traffic.

The quietest spots of all are covered in [the quietest cabins on a cruise ship](/articles/quietest-cabins-where-to-book/), and [how to read a deck plan](/articles/reading-cruise-ship-deck-plans/) shows how to check each of these before you pay.

## Then category: what each one buys
Prices vary widely by ship and season, but this is the usual ladder on a 7-night sailing, at recent prices, per person over an inside cabin.

| Category | Typical size | Usual extra vs inside | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inside | 150–170 sq ft | — | Port-heavy trips, sleeping in, tight budgets |
| Oceanview | 160–200 sq ft | US$150–400 (C$200–550) | Daylight, motion-sensitive cruisers |
| Balcony | 180–220 sq ft plus balcony | US$300–900 (C$400–1,250) | Alaska, sea days, couples |
| Suite | 250 sq ft and up | Often double a balcony | Families, perks, long trips |

More detail on layouts is in [cruise cabins explained](/articles/cruise-cabins-explained-inside-oceanview-balcony-suite/).

> **Quick math:** a couple choosing an inside cabin over a balcony on a Caribbean week might save US$1,200, roughly C$1,650. That pays for two good excursions each and a specialty dinner, on a trip where the cabin is mostly for sleeping.

## Match the cabin to the itinerary
### Caribbean from Florida
Most days are spent ashore or at the pool, so an inside or oceanview cabin is the classic smart buy. A balcony is pleasant, but you'll use it less than you think. [Inside vs balcony for a first cruise](/articles/inside-vs-balcony-first-cruise/) goes deeper.

### Alaska from Vancouver or Seattle
The scenery is the point, and glacier days and the Inside Passage pass right by your window. This is where a balcony is easiest to justify. See [the best cabins for Alaska scenery](/articles/best-cabins-alaska-cruise-scenery/).

### Long sailings and transatlantic crossings
With many sea days, the cabin becomes your living room. Space and natural light matter more.

## The traps first-timers fall into
- **Guarantee fares.** Cheaper, but the line chooses your cabin. Read [should you book a guarantee cabin](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/) first.
- **Obstructed views.** Discounted for a reason; check the specific cabin's notes and photos.
- **Connecting doors.** Handy for families, but sound passes through. Book them only if you need the door.
- **Upgrading for the wrong reason.** A suite rarely beats two balcony cabins for a family of four; compare them in [suite vs two balcony cabins](/articles/suite-vs-two-balcony-cabins/).

## What to ask before booking
1. What is directly above, below and beside this cabin number?
2. Is the view obstructed, and by what?
3. How many beds, and do they convert to a queen?
4. Is there a sofa bed or pull-down berth, and does it take floor space?

## Book the good ones early
Well-placed cabins in every category go first, especially midship balconies on Alaska sailings in July and August, when Canadian schools are out. Booking early means choosing an exact cabin number rather than taking what's left, and a licensed cruise consultant who knows the ship can flag quirks a deck plan doesn't show.
