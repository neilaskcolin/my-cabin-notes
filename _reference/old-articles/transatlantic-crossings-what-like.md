A transatlantic crossing is a run of five to eight sea days with no ports, just ocean, the ship and time. People who love it describe it as the most restful travel they've ever done. People who like a new port every day usually find it too long. Knowing which you are is most of the decision.

## What is a crossing actually like?
The first day or two feel like any sea day. By day three, a rhythm settles in: a late breakfast, a morning lecture, lunch, a walk on deck, a nap, then dinner and a show. Clocks change by an hour on most nights, so days stretch or shrink depending on your direction.

Ships schedule their fullest programmes on crossings because nobody is going ashore. Expect guest lecturers giving a series of talks, dance classes, trivia, films, cooking demonstrations and workshops.

### A typical day at sea
1. **Morning:** coffee on deck, then a lecture on maritime history or the night sky.
2. **Late morning:** a fitness class, a dance lesson or time in the library.
3. **Afternoon:** lunch, a film or a long read in a lounge chair.
4. **Early evening:** a walk around the promenade and a drink as the light fades.
5. **Night:** dinner, then a show or live music.

## Two very different ways to cross

| | Ocean liner crossing | Repositioning crossing |
|---|---|---|
| Ship | Queen Mary 2, built for the North Atlantic | Mainstream ships moving between seasons |
| Route | Usually New York to Southampton, about 7 nights | Florida or the Caribbean to Europe, or back |
| Length | About 7 nights, no ports | Often 12–16 nights, with island stops |
| When | Several times a year | Spring (to Europe) and fall (to North America) |
| Feel | Traditional, with formal evenings | Relaxed, like any sea-day-heavy cruise |
| Price | Premium | Often low per night |

Queen Mary 2 is the only purpose-built ocean liner still sailing, and it keeps traditions like formal evenings and even kennels for travelling dogs. Repositioning crossings are how most ships move between Caribbean and European seasons, often stopping in the Azores, Madeira or the Canary Islands. For more on those, see [what a repositioning cruise is](/articles/repositioning-cruises-explained/).

## Will it be rough?
This is open ocean, not the Inside Passage. Spring and fall crossings are more likely to see swells, and northern routes are cooler and windier. Southern routes via the Azores or Canaries are usually milder.

Book a midship cabin on a lower deck, pack the seasickness remedies that work for you and take them early. [How bad seasickness really gets](/articles/seasickness-how-bad-really/) covers the options.

> **Canadian tip:** you'll be several days from land, so medical help is limited to the ship's medical centre. Your provincial health plan won't cover care at sea, and evacuation can be very costly. Buy travel medical insurance with evacuation cover, and check any age limits on your policy.

## Who loves crossings and who doesn't
They suit readers, people who love being at sea, couples who want unhurried time together, and travellers heading to Europe for a longer stay anyway. Westbound crossings are also a gentle way to come home without jet lag.

They are a poor fit for anyone who gets restless without ports, is very prone to seasickness, or wants warm weather and pool days the whole way.

## Getting there and home from Canada
Crossings are one-way, so you will fly the other direction. Air Canada, WestJet and Air Transat serve many European cities from Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver, with connections from Calgary, Edmonton, Winnipeg and Halifax. A popular plan is to sail to Europe in spring, tour by land for a week or two, then fly home.

A few northern crossings call in Iceland, Greenland or Atlantic Canada, which can shorten the journey for some Canadians. Confirm routes for your dates.
