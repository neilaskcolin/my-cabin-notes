For most families of four, two balcony cabins come out cheaper than a suite and more practical day to day. But the gap varies hugely by line and sailing, and on some dates the suite costs barely more. The only reliable answer is a side-by-side price test on your own dates.

## What each option really gives you
Two connecting or adjacent balconies give you two bathrooms, four proper beds, two balconies and a door you can close at bedtime. For a family with a morning rush or teenagers, that is a lot of value.

A suite gives you one larger shared space, usually a separate sleeping area, a bigger balcony and a stack of perks. These might include priority boarding, a suite lounge or restaurant, a concierge, and on some lines included drinks, wifi or specialty dining. Some family suites have a second bathroom, but many do not.

## Putting a value on suite perks
The perks are where suite pricing makes sense or falls apart. Value them at what you would actually spend, not what they cost à la carte.

| Suite perk | Worth money to you if… | Rough value for four over a week |
|---|---|---|
| Included specialty dining | You would book two or three paid dinners anyway | Often US$300–700 |
| Included drinks or wifi | Adults would buy packages anyway | Can exceed US$500, depending on the line |
| Priority boarding and tendering | You hate queues or have young kids | No cash value, real comfort |
| Suite lounge or restaurant | You'd use it most days | Hard to price |
| Larger living area | You spend time in the cabin together | Depends on your family |
| Concierge help | You want reservations handled for you | Small |

Perk bundles vary a lot between suite programs, so check exactly what your suite category includes. Entry-level suites on some lines include little more than a bigger cabin.

> **Example:** a Toronto family with kids aged 9 and 13 prices a 7-night Caribbean sailing. Two balconies come to about US$5,200 for four. A family suite, with lower fares for the third and fourth guests, comes to about US$6,600, plus roughly US$80 more in gratuities. That is a gap of about US$1,500, or around C$2,000. If the suite includes perks they would use worth US$600, the real premium is closer to US$900.

## How to run the price test
1. Price two balconies, connecting if available, adjacent if not, for your exact sailing.
2. Price the suite for four, with taxes and fees.
3. Add the difference in gratuities, since suite rates are often higher.
4. Subtract the value of suite perks you would genuinely use.
5. Compare the real difference against what you get: bathrooms and separation versus space and perks.

## Other set-ups worth pricing
- **A balcony for the parents and an inside cabin across the hall** for the kids, often the cheapest way to get two bathrooms.
- **A single four-berth balcony or family cabin,** cheapest of all if your kids are young and you all sleep easily.
- **Connecting cabins in different categories** where the ship's layout allows it.

Connecting cabins are scarce and sell early, so book well ahead if you want them. [Connecting cabins for families](/articles/connecting-cabins-one-big-cabin-families/) explains how they work.

> **Watch out:** most lines require children in their own cabin to be next to, connecting to or near a parent's cabin, and some set minimum ages. Book both cabins together and confirm the arrangement in writing.

## The teenager variable
Teenagers usually love having their own space, which favours two cabins. Families with toddlers often prefer one space so a parent can stay close during naps and bedtime, which favours a suite or a single family cabin. ## Which wins for your family
On most mainstream sailings, two balconies win on price and practicality. A suite earns its place when the gap is small, the perks match how you holiday, or you value time together in one large space. Run the test on two or three candidate sailings, because the answer can flip from one week to the next.
