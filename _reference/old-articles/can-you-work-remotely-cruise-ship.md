You can, and it works far better than it did a few years ago. Most of the big lines have added low-orbit satellite internet, typically Starlink, to much of their fleets, so email, chat and most video calls are realistic. The catch is that you're paying hotel-style prices for it, and the ship's schedule, not your calendar, decides when you're free.

## How good is cruise ship wifi for work now?
On an upgraded ship, the top tier is usually good enough for a normal desk day: email, shared documents, Teams or Zoom with your camera on, and a VPN. Speeds dip when thousands of passengers log on after dinner, and very large uploads still crawl compared with home fibre.

Older ships that haven't been upgraded, and some smaller or expedition vessels, can still be patchy. Ask which system your specific ship uses before you count on it. [How cruise ship internet works](/articles/cruise-ship-internet-how-it-works/) explains the technology and why speeds vary.

## Which wifi package do you need for remote work?

| Your workload | Tier to buy | Realistic? |
|---|---|---|
| Email and messaging only | Basic or "surf" | Yes, on almost any ship |
| Shared docs, VPN, web apps | Standard or premium | Yes, on upgraded ships |
| Video calls with camera | Premium or streaming | Usually, with a backup plan |
| Large uploads, live streaming | Premium, off-peak hours | Possible, but slow |

Packages run roughly US$15–40 per device per day at recent prices, and the work-capable tier sits at the top of that range. Some lines bundle wifi into certain fares, which changes the maths. Each line's packages are in [cruise wifi and phone roaming for Canadians](/articles/cruise-wifi-phone-roaming-canadians/).

> **Canadian tip:** put your phone in airplane mode before sail-away. Canadian carrier travel plans generally don't cover the ship's maritime cell network, and a phone quietly syncing work email at sea can run up a painful bill.

## Where to actually work on the ship
Cabins are fine for focused work, but signal is often weaker in interior cabins down long corridors. For calls, look for:

- **The library or card room:** quiet, near access points and empty on port days.
- **A lounge before noon:** many bars sit idle until the afternoon.
- **Your balcony:** good for thinking, poor for screens in bright sun and noisy in wind.
- **Port cafés and libraries:** in Alaska ports, Juneau and Ketchikan are often easy places to find free wifi ashore.

Bring headphones with a decent microphone. Ship announcements and the background hum of a busy atrium carry on calls more than you'd expect.

## Time zones and your meeting calendar
Ships change clocks as they move, and your colleagues won't. A Toronto worker on an Alaska cruise from Vancouver is three hours behind the office, then four once the ship switches to Alaska time. A 9 a.m. Eastern stand-up becomes a 5 a.m. alarm.

Caribbean sailings from Florida are kinder, since Fort Lauderdale and Miami share Eastern time with Toronto and Montreal, though some islands sit an hour off. [Ship's time and time zones](/articles/cruise-cabin-jet-lag-time-zones/) shows where the clock jumps happen.

### Pick the right itinerary
- **Sea-day-heavy sailings** such as transatlantic crossings or longer repositioning cruises give long, predictable work blocks. [Repositioning cruises explained](/articles/repositioning-cruises-explained/) covers how they work.
- **Port-intensive weeks** like a typical Alaska or Western Caribbean itinerary leave little working time if you also want to go ashore.
- **Scenic days**, such as glacier cruising, are a poor choice for meetings. You'll want to be on deck.

## Check with your employer first
This is the step people skip. Many Canadian employers' remote-work policies limit working from outside the country because of data security, insurance or tax rules, and a ship in US or international waters counts as outside Canada. Some IT departments also block logins from unexpected locations.

Ask in writing before you book, and test your VPN and work apps on the first afternoon while you're still near port.

## Is it worth combining work and a cruise?
It suits people with flexible hours, mostly asynchronous work, and a few sea days to fill. It suits people tied to a fixed meeting schedule, client calls or tight deadlines much less. A common middle ground is taking a cruise with a couple of light "check-in" days rather than a full working week.

## A simple plan for a working week at sea
Buy the premium tier for one device, block calls for sea days and mornings, keep a dial-in number as backup, and tell colleagues your new time zone before you go. If connectivity is truly critical, confirm your specific ship's internet system before booking rather than relying on the line's general marketing.
