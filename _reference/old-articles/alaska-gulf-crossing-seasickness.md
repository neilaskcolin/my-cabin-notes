Yes, it can be rougher than the rest of an Alaska cruise, but "rough" is relative. On a modern big ship in summer, most passengers notice a gentle roll for a day and carry on. If you are prone to motion sickness, a little planning makes it far easier.

## Where the open water actually is
Most of a Vancouver departure is sheltered. Here is where you feel the ocean on each type of sailing:

| Stretch | Which sailings | Exposure | How long |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inside Passage channels | All Vancouver sailings | Sheltered by islands | Most of the trip |
| Queen Charlotte Sound | Vancouver sailings, north of Vancouver Island | Partly open to the Pacific | A few hours |
| Outside Vancouver Island | Most Seattle round trips | Open Pacific | Much of the first and last day |
| Gulf of Alaska | One-way sailings to or from Seward or Whittier | Open ocean | Around a day in total |

For the calmer round-trip option, see [seasickness on the Inside Passage](/articles/seasickness-inside-passage/). For the route choice itself, see [Inside Passage vs Gulf of Alaska](/articles/inside-passage-vs-gulf-of-alaska/).

## What the Gulf crossing feels like
Expect a slow, rolling motion rather than sharp slamming. You may feel the ship pitch gently when walking forward, see some glasses sway at the bar, and sleep a little lighter. The pool may close if water sloshes. Captains also adjust course and speed for comfort when they can.

Severe conditions do happen, most often outside midsummer. Stabilizers on large ships reduce rolling a lot, though they do less for the up-and-down pitching felt at the bow.

## When you will meet it
On a northbound cruise from Vancouver, the Gulf comes in the last two or three days, after you have your sea legs. Southbound passengers usually meet it within the first day or two. [Northbound vs southbound](/articles/alaska-cruise-northern-vs-southbound-embarkation/) covers the other differences between directions.

## How to prepare
- **Pick the right cabin.** Low and midship moves least. A high forward cabin feels the most motion.
- **Start remedies early.** Take medication before the crossing begins, not once you feel ill. Check timing with the ship's daily schedule.
- **Use the horizon.** Fresh air on a lower open deck, looking at the horizon, helps more than lying in an inside cabin.
- **Eat lightly and often.** Crackers, bread and apples sit better than rich food or a lot of alcohol.
- **Have a backup.** Guest services often keep basic seasickness tablets, and the medical centre can give injections for bad cases, at a charge.

> **Canadian tip:** dimenhydrinate (sold in Canada as Gravol, which also comes in a ginger version) is available without a prescription and is easy to pack. Scopolamine patches usually need a prescription, so talk to your doctor before you go. Note that your provincial health plan won't pay for onboard medical care; see [cruise medical care costs](/articles/cruise-medical-care-costs/).

## Should seasickness change your itinerary?
For most people, no. One day of moderate swell is a fair trade for Hubbard Glacier, College Fjord and the chance to add Denali. If you have been badly seasick on ferries or previous cruises, a round-trip Inside Passage sailing from Vancouver is the gentler choice, and you can still fly to Anchorage separately for a land trip.
