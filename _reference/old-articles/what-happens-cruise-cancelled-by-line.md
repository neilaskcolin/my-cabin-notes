If the cruise line cancels your sailing, you get your money back. Expect a full refund of what you paid the line, usually with the option of a future cruise credit instead. What the line does not owe you is the flight, hotel and car rental you booked yourself, and that gap is where Canadian travellers lose money.

## Why do cruise lines cancel sailings?
Full cancellations are uncommon, and most have one of a handful of causes:

- Mechanical trouble that needs unscheduled drydock time.
- A ship being sold, moved to another brand or redeployed to a different region.
- Severe weather, such as a hurricane sitting over the departure port.
- A port or region closing, or a public health order. The closure of Canadian waters to large cruise ships in 2020 and 2021 wiped out two full Alaska seasons from Vancouver.

Redeployments and sales usually come with weeks or months of notice. Weather cancellations can come with only days.

## What you get when the line cancels
Exact terms vary by line and by the reason, so treat this as the typical pattern and confirm against your cancellation notice.

| What happens | What you normally get | What you usually don't get |
|---|---|---|
| Whole sailing cancelled | Full refund to original payment, or a future cruise credit, often with a bonus | Money back for flights and hotels booked elsewhere, unless the line offers it |
| Sailing shortened | A partial refund or credit for the lost days | Compensation for missed connections |
| Port skipped or swapped | Refund of port charges for that stop, sometimes onboard credit | Any refund of the cruise fare |
| Ship excursion cancelled | Refund to your onboard account or card | Compensation for a tour you booked independently |

Extras you prepaid through the line, such as drink packages, wifi, specialty dining, prepaid gratuities and ship excursions, are normally refunded along with the fare.

### Refund or future cruise credit?
Lines often offer a credit worth more than the cash refund to keep your business. That can be the better deal, but only if you will genuinely sail with the same line before the credit expires. Credits usually carry an expiry date, may not cover taxes and fees, and are normally tied to the original guests. The [future cruise credit guide](/articles/future-cruise-credit-explained/) covers the fine print.

> **Example:** a Toronto couple paid C$5,000 for a cancelled Caribbean sailing and are offered either C$5,000 back or a 125% credit worth C$6,250. If they already planned to sail with that line next winter, the credit is worth an extra C$1,250. If they are unsure, the cash keeps every option open.

## What about flights and hotels you booked yourself?
This is the part that hurts. If you bought flights through the cruise line, the line normally cancels and refunds or rebooks them. If you booked your own, the line has no obligation, which is the main argument in [cruise line air vs booking your own flights](/articles/cruise-line-air-vs-own-flights/).

With your own Air Canada or WestJet tickets, what you recover depends on the fare type. Flexible fares can be refunded, many standard fares give a travel credit, and the cheapest basic fares may give you very little. Some lines offer to reimburse airline change fees up to a cap in their cancellation notice, so read it before you call the airline. Hotels on refundable rates are easy; prepaid rates usually are not.

Travel insurance can fill the gap, but only if the reason is covered. Check whether your policy's trip cancellation section lists cancellation by the cruise line or travel supplier as a covered reason, and what it pays when the supplier itself refunds the fare.

> **Canadian tip:** pay for the cruise with a credit card. If a promised refund never arrives, the card's dispute process is a backstop, and some Canadian premium cards include trip cancellation cover when the trip is charged to them.

## What to do when the cancellation notice arrives
1. Read the whole notice, including the deadline to choose a refund or credit and what happens if you do not reply.
2. Check whether the line is offering anything towards flights or hotels, and what proof it wants.
3. Contact your airline and hotel to cancel or change, keeping every receipt and email.
4. Open an insurance claim if your policy might respond, with the written cancellation attached.
5. Note the date the line quoted for the refund and follow up if it passes.

If you booked through an agency, it handles most of this paperwork for you.

## Itinerary changes are far more common
Most disruptions are not cancellations at all. The [cruise contract of carriage](/articles/cruise-contract-of-carriage-explained/) lets the line change ports for weather, safety or operational reasons. On an Alaska sailing, ice can block a fjord and the ship swaps to a different glacier route. In the Caribbean, ships reroute around storms, as covered in [what happens if a hurricane threatens your cruise](/articles/what-happens-hurricane-cruise-caribbean/). You sail as planned, minus a port, and usually get back only the unused port charges.

## Protecting a booking before anything goes wrong
Buy insurance when you pay the deposit, not at final payment. Choose flexible flights or cruise line air where the price gap is small, and fly in the day before. The comparison of [travel insurance vs cruise line protection plans](/articles/travel-insurance-vs-cruise-protection/) explains which policy fills which gap.
