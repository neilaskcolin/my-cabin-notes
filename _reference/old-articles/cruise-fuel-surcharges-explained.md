Fuel surcharges still exist on paper but rarely in practice. Most cruise contracts keep a clause that would allow a capped supplement if oil prices spike, yet mainstream lines have largely stopped using it, choosing to raise fares for new bookings instead.

## A short history
When oil prices climbed steeply in the late 2000s, several big lines added fuel supplements, typically a few dollars per person per day, and in some cases applied them to passengers who had already booked. That caused a wave of complaints and regulatory attention, and some lines backed away from charging existing bookings.

Since then, fuel costs have mostly been folded into the fare. Even through later spikes in oil prices, mainstream lines have generally preferred raising prices for new bookings over reviving separate surcharges.

## What the contract clause usually contains
The wording differs by line, but fuel clauses tend to share the same parts:

| Part of the clause | What it typically says |
|---|---|
| Trigger | A benchmark crude oil price above a stated level |
| Amount | A per-person, per-day supplement |
| Cap | A maximum per day, sometimes also per voyage |
| Who pays | May include passengers already booked, unless the line says otherwise |
| Timing | Can be imposed with notice, sometimes even after final payment |

You can find yours by searching the ticket contract for "fuel". [The contract of carriage explained](/articles/cruise-contract-of-carriage-explained/) shows what else is worth reading while you are there.

## How much could it cost?
The exposure is small compared with other costs. If a clause allowed, say, US$10 per person per day, a couple on a 7-night cruise would pay US$140, roughly C$190–195. Annoying, but not a trip-breaker.

> **Good to know:** for Canadians, currency movement is a bigger and far more common risk than a fuel surcharge. A few cents' shift in the loonie on a US-dollar booking or onboard bill can easily cost more than a capped supplement. [How exchange rates affect cruise pricing](/articles/exchange-rate-cruise-pricing-canadians/) explains how to manage it.

## Fuel surcharges vs other charges
- **Taxes, fees and port charges** are set by governments and ports, and most contracts let lines pass on increases. See [taxes and port fees explained](/articles/taxes-port-fees-cruise-fare-breakdown/).
- **Daily gratuities** go to crew and can rise if you have not prepaid.
- **A fuel supplement** would be a separate, clearly labelled charge tied to oil prices.

The broader question of what can change after you book is covered in [can a cruise line raise your fare after booking](/articles/can-cruise-line-raise-fare-after-booking/).

## What to do if a surcharge is ever announced
- Ask whether it applies to existing bookings or only new ones.
- Ask whether you can prepay it at the current rate.
- Check whether your travel agent or the line will waive it as goodwill.
- Keep the announcement and your booking confirmation in case you need to dispute it.

## Should it affect your booking?
No. Read the fuel clause once so you know the trigger and the cap, then put your attention on things that matter more: a refundable fare, prepaid gratuities and a sensible plan for currency. If you want certainty, ask the line or your agent whether any surcharge is currently in force before you pay.
