Every large cruise ship has a small medical centre with a doctor and nurses, and it can handle far more than seasickness. What surprises Canadians is the bill: you pay private rates, charged to your cabin, and your provincial plan will not pick up much of it.

## What can the ship's medical centre actually treat?
Think of it as a well-equipped walk-in clinic with some emergency-room capability. A typical big-ship centre has one or two doctors, a few nurses, an examination room or two, a small pharmacy, basic lab tests, an ECG, oxygen and a couple of beds for observation. Many lines model their facilities on cruise medicine guidelines published by emergency physicians in the US.

Common reasons people visit:
- Seasickness that pills haven't fixed (an injection often works quickly)
- Stomach bugs, colds, chest infections and ear problems
- Sprains, cuts needing stitches, and falls on wet decks
- Flare-ups of existing conditions, such as asthma or blood pressure

What it can't do is replace a hospital. There is no surgery, no specialist and usually no CT scanner. For a suspected heart attack, stroke, serious fracture or appendicitis, the team stabilizes you and arranges to get you ashore.

## What does cruise ship medical care cost?
Fees vary by line and are not always published, so treat these as rough illustrative ranges at recent prices. Bills are in US dollars.

| Situation | Typical charge (USD) | Roughly in CAD |
|---|---|---|
| Consultation during clinic hours | $100–200 | C$140–280 |
| After-hours or cabin call | $150–300 | C$210–420 |
| Seasickness injection plus consult | $150–250 | C$210–350 |
| Cut needing stitches, with dressings | $300–800 | C$420–1,100 |
| Overnight observation with IV and tests | $1,000–3,000+ | C$1,400–4,200+ |
| Medical evacuation and shore hospital | tens of thousands | tens of thousands |

> **Good to know:** many lines hand out basic seasickness tablets free at guest services, and some waive the consultation fee for suspected stomach bugs because they want sick passengers to come forward. Ask before you assume you'll be charged.

### Why evacuation is the real risk
Clinic visits are annoying but survivable. The expensive scenario is being taken off the ship by helicopter or tender, admitted to a hospital in Alaska, Mexico or the Caribbean, then flown home by air ambulance. A US hospital stay alone can run into five figures, and an air ambulance back to Canada adds more on top.

## Why your provincial plan won't cover it
Provincial health plans are built for care in Canada. Once the ship leaves Canadian waters, the clinic counts as an out-of-country provider. Most provinces pay nothing or a small amount at Canadian rates, which barely dents a US-style bill. Even a round trip from Vancouver is out of country for almost the whole week.

The fix is private travel medical insurance. [Travel insurance for cruises](/articles/travel-insurance-cruise-canadians/) covers what to check: a high emergency medical limit (C$5 million or more is common), evacuation and repatriation, and no exclusion for cruising.

> **Watch out:** credit card travel medical cover often has age limits, a cap on trip length, and conditions such as charging the trip to that card. Read the certificate before relying on it, especially if you are over 60.

If you have a managed condition, the stability clause detail is in [cruise insurance for travellers over 60](/articles/cruise-insurance-seniors-canada-pre-existing/).

## What to do if you need care on board
1. **Call your insurer's assistance line first** if it isn't an emergency. Many policies want to be told before treatment, and they can talk to the ship's doctor.
2. **Go to the medical centre during clinic hours** where you can. After-hours calls cost more.
3. **Ask for a cost estimate** before tests or procedures that aren't urgent.
4. **Get an itemized bill and a medical report** before you leave the ship. Insurers rarely pay without them.
5. **Keep pharmacy receipts** and note the names of any drugs given.
6. **File the claim promptly** once home, with your boarding documents as proof of travel dates.

> **Example:** a Winnipeg couple on a 7-night Caribbean cruise, where one partner slips on the pool deck and needs stitches, might see a bill of US$500–700 on the final statement. With insurance, they pay it on the card, then claim it back. Without, they absorb roughly C$700–1,000.

## How to lower the odds of a medical bill
Pack more medication than you need, in original containers, in your carry-on. Bring your own seasickness remedies and start them early (see [how bad seasickness really is](/articles/seasickness-how-bad-really/)). Wash hands often and use the sanitizer stations, since stomach bugs spread fast in a closed community. Wear proper shoes on wet decks.

Answer the [pre-boarding health questionnaire](/articles/cruise-ship-medical-form-health-questionnaire/) honestly, and if you feel unwell before you leave home, read [what to do if you're sick the day before a cruise](/articles/what-if-you-get-sick-day-before-cruise/).

## Making sure you're actually covered
The ship's clinic is good at what it does, but it is a private service with private prices, and evacuation is where the real money goes. Buy medical insurance that names cruising, check its evacuation limit, and keep the policy number and assistance line on your phone and on paper while you sail.
