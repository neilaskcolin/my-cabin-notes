Cruise ships can work very well for travellers with hearing or vision loss, but the useful equipment is usually requested, not automatic. The single best step is to contact the line's accessibility team as soon as you book. Mobility needs have their own guide in [accessible cruising for mobility](/articles/accessible-cruising-mobility-ships/).

## What ships provide for hearing loss
### In the cabin
The core piece is an alert system that turns sound into light or vibration. Depending on the line, this is either a dedicated accessible cabin or a portable kit delivered to a standard cabin. Typical kits include:
- A flashing or vibrating door-knock sensor
- A visual phone-ring signal
- A strobe smoke alarm and a bed shaker for emergency alerts
- A TV with closed captioning, which most modern ships offer on cabin screens

### Around the ship
Some lines offer assistive listening devices or hearing loops in the main theatre, and a few provide sign language interpretation of headline shows or port talks on request. These usually need weeks of notice, not days, so ask at booking. Shore excursion guides rarely have amplification, so tours with small groups work better.

> **Good to know:** sea air is humid. Bring spare hearing aid batteries or your charger, a drying case, and a copy of your audiologist's details. Ships' shops may not stock your battery size.

## What ships provide for low vision or blindness
### On board
- Braille or tactile numbers at lifts and often at cabin doors
- Large-print or braille menus and daily programmes on request
- An orientation walk from crew on boarding day, if arranged ahead
- Screen-reader support in some lines' apps, which varies widely

Big ships are mazes of similar corridors. Choosing a cabin near a lift bank and learning one route to the dining room and one to your muster station makes the week easier. The tips in [finding your way around a big ship](/articles/navigating-large-cruise-ship-without-getting-lost/) help here too.

### In port
Tender ports, with their moving boats and steep steps, are the hardest stops for low-vision travellers. Check the itinerary for tender stops and read [how tenders work](/articles/cruise-ship-tenders-explained/) so you can decide whether to stay aboard.

## Service dogs: the rules that catch people out
Trained guide and hearing dogs are generally accepted, and the ship supplies a relief area, often a box of mulch or turf on an outer deck. Emotional support animals usually are not. The harder part is the ports: every country on the route sets its own animal entry rules, and some islands make it close to impossible.

> **Watch out:** the US tightened dog entry rules in 2024, including age and microchip requirements and an online import form. Check current CDC and port rules, plus CFIA rules for bringing your dog home to Canada, months before you sail.

## How to request accommodations, step by step
1. Before paying a deposit, email or call the line's accessibility department and ask exactly what your ship offers.
2. At booking, list every need: alert kit, captioning, interpreter, large print, service dog, orientation.
3. Get written confirmation, and keep it with your travel documents.
4. Ask the line to note you on the safety list, so crew know to check on you if an alarm sounds.
5. Request boarding assistance at the terminal, whether Canada Place in Vancouver or a Florida port.
6. On day one, check the kit works and tell guest services anything missing.

Lines generally expect guests who need personal help to travel with a companion, since crew do not provide personal care. Flying to the port? On domestic Canadian flights with large carriers, the One Person, One Fare rule can mean a needed attendant flies free, so ask when you book your Toronto–Vancouver or Calgary–Vancouver leg.

## Before you book
Declare any hearing or vision condition on your travel insurance, as covered in [insurance with pre-existing conditions](/articles/cruise-insurance-seniors-canada-pre-existing/), and treat medical needs the same way you would [dietary needs](/articles/dietary-needs-cruise-ships/): flagged early and confirmed in writing. A licensed cruise consultant can help compare what each ship offers before you commit.
