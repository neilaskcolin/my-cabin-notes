At a tender port, the ship drops anchor offshore and small boats carry passengers to the pier. It adds a short boat ride and a queue to your morning, and it's the one type of port where weather can stop you going ashore at all.

## Why do some ports need tenders?
Some harbours are too shallow for a big ship, have no pier long enough, or have more ships than berths on a busy day. Rather than skip the port, the ship anchors and runs a shuttle. Tender boats are often the ship's own lifeboats, built to carry over 100 people, though some ports use local boats instead.

Ports you'll commonly tender into:

| Port | Region | Note |
|---|---|---|
| George Town, Grand Cayman | Western Caribbean | Busy; weather sometimes stops tenders |
| Belize City | Western Caribbean | Ride can be longer, as ships anchor well offshore |
| Bar Harbor, Maine | New England and Canada | Common on fall foliage sailings |
| Santorini | Greek Islands | Then a cable car or donkey path up the cliff |
| Villefranche, for Nice | French Riviera | Often tendered |
| Sitka, Alaska | Alaska | Some ships dock out of town, others tender in |

Most Alaska and many Caribbean ports have proper piers, so a typical 7-night sailing has none or one tender port. Fall sailings to Atlantic Canada often include Bar Harbor (see [New England and Canada fall cruises](/articles/new-england-canada-fall-cruises/)).

## How the tender queue works

### Morning rush
On arrival, ship excursion groups go first because their tours are timed. Everyone else collects a tender ticket, usually in the theatre or a lounge, and waits for their number to be called. On some ships, tickets are issued through the app.

### Skip the queue
If you're not on a timed tour, have breakfast and go an hour or two after arrival. By then most ships switch to walk-on boarding, and the wait is often minutes.

### The ride
Crew help you step from a platform low on the ship onto the boat. The ride typically takes 10–20 minutes. Hold handrails and keep a hand free while stepping across.

> **Watch out:** the last tender back usually leaves around 30 minutes before all-aboard, and the queue at the pier can be long in the final hour. If you miss it, the ship will not wait (see [what happens if you miss the ship in port](/articles/what-happens-miss-ship-port/)).

## Mobility considerations
Stepping between a moving platform and a moving boat is the hard part. Many lines can manage passengers who can take a few steps with help, and some carry folding wheelchairs aboard the tender. Scooters and heavy powered chairs often can't be carried, and the officer in charge may restrict access on choppy days.

If mobility is a factor:
- Tell guest services at boarding which tender ports are on your itinerary
- Bring a folding manual wheelchair as a backup for tender days
- Ask whether a lift-equipped tender or a dock at a nearby pier is available
- Book docked ports for the excursions that matter most

The [accessible cruising guide](/articles/accessible-cruising-mobility-ships/) and [wheelchair-accessible Alaska cruises](/articles/wheelchair-accessible-alaska-cruises/) go further.

## When weather cancels tendering
Tendering needs reasonably calm water at the ship's side. In strong wind or swell, the captain can delay operations or cancel the call. You'll usually get a refund of that port's taxes and fees, and ship-booked excursions are refunded automatically. Independent tours are a separate matter, so book with operators that refund when the ship can't call (see [ship excursions versus independent tours](/articles/cruise-excursion-through-ship-vs-independent/)).

## Planning around tender ports on your itinerary
Check which ports on your itinerary are tender stops, avoid booking tight independent tours for the first hour, and allow plenty of time to return. Learning the other bits of ship jargon helps too: see [lido, promenade and more ship terms](/articles/cruise-ship-terminology-lido-promenade-deck/).
