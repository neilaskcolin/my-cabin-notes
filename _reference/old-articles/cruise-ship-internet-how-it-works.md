Cruise ship internet works by satellite. The ship has dish or flat-panel antennas on its upper decks, these talk to satellites, and the satellites relay your data to ground stations on land. How good it feels depends on which satellites, how much bandwidth the line has bought, and how many other people are streaming at the same time.

## Why ship internet used to be so bad
For years, ships relied on geostationary satellites parked about 36,000 km above the equator. A signal travelling that far up and back adds a noticeable delay, so video calls stuttered and web pages loaded in slow motion, even when the bandwidth itself was fine.

## What changed with Starlink
Since around 2022, most of the big lines have added low-orbit satellite service, mainly Starlink, to most or all of their ships. Those satellites orbit only a few hundred kilometres up, so the delay shrinks to something close to home broadband.

| | Older geostationary systems | Low-orbit systems such as Starlink |
|---|---|---|
| Satellite height | About 36,000 km | A few hundred km |
| Delay you notice | Long; calls lag | Short; calls usually work |
| Video streaming | Patchy or blocked | Usually possible on premium tiers |
| Weak spots | Bad weather, heavy use | Some remote or polar waters, heavy use |

Ships typically blend several links, and in some ports the ship may switch systems or limit service. Expect good, not perfect.

## Why speeds still vary during the week
- **Evenings are busiest.** Thousands of people log on after dinner. Early mornings and port days are faster.
- **Remote waters.** Coverage can thin in some far-flung areas and in narrow fjords with steep walls.
- **Where you sit.** Thick steel walls and long corridors weaken the ship's wifi signal; lounges near access points work best.
- **Your tier.** Cheap "social" or "surf" plans are capped or throttled. Streaming tiers get more bandwidth.

## How packages are sold
Most lines charge per device per day, roughly US$15–40 at recent prices, with discounts if you buy before you sail or buy for several devices. Some fares bundle wifi, and some suites include it. You usually log in with your cabin number and birth date, and some packages let you switch which device is connected.

## How to get online without overspending
1. **Turn off cellular data and roaming** before the ship leaves port, so your phone doesn't connect to the ship's expensive maritime cell network.
2. **Decide who really needs it.** One shared device on a cheaper plan may be enough for a couple.
3. **Buy before sailing** if the line offers a pre-cruise discount.
4. **Download before you go:** films, playlists, maps and boarding documents.
5. **Do heavy tasks early in the morning** or while most passengers are ashore.

> **Canadian tip:** in Alaska ports you're on US cell networks, so a carrier US day pass, typically around C$12–16 a day, can be cheaper than a day of ship wifi. Turn it on only in port, and back to airplane mode before sailing. Full detail is in [cruise wifi and phone roaming for Canadians](/articles/cruise-wifi-phone-roaming-canadians/).

## Choosing the right connectivity setup for your trip
Work out what you actually need online, a few messages a day or a daily video call, and buy the cheapest tier that does it. Ship internet is now good enough for most people, but it's still a shared connection in the middle of the ocean.
