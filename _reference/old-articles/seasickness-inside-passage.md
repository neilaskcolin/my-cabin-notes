If seasickness is what's keeping you off a cruise, Alaska's Inside Passage is the route to try. Islands block the open Pacific for most of the way, and on many days the only sign you're moving is the scenery sliding past the window.

## Why the Inside Passage is so calm

The route threads through channels between the BC and Alaska mainland and a long chain of islands. Big swells from the open ocean rarely reach these waters. Large ships have stabilizers too, which damp most of the roll that's left. Many passengers who describe themselves as poor sailors cross this coast without a twinge.

## Where you might feel motion, section by section

Routes vary by ship and weather, but on a typical Vancouver sailing the picture looks like this:

| Stretch | How exposed | What you might feel |
|---|---|---|
| Strait of Georgia, leaving Vancouver | Sheltered | Almost nothing |
| Inside of Vancouver Island | Sheltered | Almost nothing |
| Queen Charlotte Sound | Open to the Pacific for a few hours | Gentle swell, occasionally noticeable |
| Northern BC coast and Dixon Entrance | Partly open in places | Short spells of motion in poor weather |
| Southeast Alaska channels and fjords | Very sheltered | Flat, river-like water |
| Gulf of Alaska (one-ways only) | Open ocean for around a day | The trip's likeliest rolling |

The open stretches are often crossed overnight, so many people sleep straight through them.

## Route choice is your biggest lever

If anyone in your group is motion-sensitive, choose the round trip from Vancouver. It stays in sheltered water nearly all the way and skips the Gulf of Alaska entirely. The one-way's Gulf day is usually comfortable, but it is the part of an Alaska cruise most likely to cause trouble, as explained in [Alaska Gulf crossing seasickness](/articles/alaska-gulf-crossing-seasickness/). The full route comparison is in [Inside Passage vs Gulf of Alaska](/articles/inside-passage-vs-gulf-of-alaska/).

## The prevention plan that works

1. **Book midship, low to mid deck.** It's the steadiest part of the ship. Forward and high cabins move the most.
2. **Pack remedies even if you think you won't need them.** Tablets, acupressure bands or a prescription patch are cheap insurance.
3. **Take tablets before the open stretch, not after.** Remedies work far better as prevention. Check the daily programme for the overnight route and take a dose at bedtime if you're crossing Queen Charlotte Sound.
4. **If you feel queasy, go midship and low, get fresh air and look at the horizon.** Avoid reading or screens until it passes.
5. **Eat a little.** Plain crackers and green apples, the classic crew remedies, help many people more than an empty stomach.

> **Canadian tip:** Gravol, including its less drowsy ginger version, is available at any Canadian pharmacy and is easy to pack. Prescription patches need a doctor's visit, so book one well before the trip if you want them. Ask your pharmacist which option suits any other medication you take.

Ships also sell motion-sickness tablets on board, and some hand them out at guest services, but don't count on it. For more general advice, see [seasickness on a cruise: how bad is it really](/articles/seasickness-how-bad-really/), and if you're sailing with children, [seasickness in kids](/articles/seasickness-in-kids-what-to-do/).

> **Good to know:** glacier days are the calmest of all. Fjords and bays like Endicott Arm and Glacier Bay are usually flat, and the ship moves at a crawl.

## The bottom line

Choose the sheltered round trip, ask for a midship cabin on a lower-to-middle deck, and carry remedies you probably won't open. A specific cabin you've chosen is better than one assigned at random, which is one reason to avoid a [guarantee cabin](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/) if motion worries you.
