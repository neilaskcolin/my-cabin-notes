A Galápagos cruise is expensive and takes some effort to reach from Canada, but nothing else gets you as close to wildlife that simply isn't afraid of people. Here's how the trip works, what it really costs, and who it suits.

## Why every Galápagos cruise is a small ship
Ecuador's national park rules cap ships at 100 passengers, assign each vessel a fixed itinerary, and limit visitor groups to about 16 people per licensed naturalist guide. You can't wander ashore on your own at visitor sites; you land, walk marked trails and snorkel with your guide.

Vessels range from 16-guest yachts to 100-guest expedition ships run by lines such as Celebrity, Silversea and Lindblad. Bigger ships are steadier and more comfortable; small yachts are more intimate and often cheaper. [Best small-ship lines for wildlife and nature lovers](/articles/best-small-ship-lines-wildlife-nature/) compares the styles.

## What the days look like
Expect two outings a day: a morning landing and an afternoon snorkel or zodiac ride, with lunch and a rest while the ship moves. Typical sightings include giant tortoises, marine iguanas, sea lions, blue-footed boobies, frigatebirds and, in the water, turtles, rays and reef sharks. Evenings bring a briefing for the next day. It's busy, early to bed and very different from a big-ship cruise.

## When to go
- **December to May (warm season):** air around the high 20s °C, warmer water, calmer seas and green islands, with short tropical showers.
- **June to November (cool season):** cooler, often misty days, water cool enough for a wetsuit, and choppier seas, but rich marine life thanks to the cold currents.
- **Canadian holidays:** Christmas, New Year and March break departures sell out first and cost the most.

## Getting there from Canada
There are no nonstop flights from Canada. Most travellers connect through Miami, Houston, Panama City or Bogotá to Quito or Guayaquil, then fly about two hours to Baltra or San Cristóbal. Quito sits at about 2,850 metres, so some people prefer Guayaquil, at sea level, for the overnight.

Arrive on the mainland at least a day early. Ships won't wait for a missed connection, the same principle as [flying the day before any cruise](/articles/fly-day-before-cruise/).

### Documents and money
Canadians don't need a visa for a tourist visit to Ecuador, but your passport should be valid for well beyond the trip; six months is the safe rule. Ecuador uses the US dollar, so bring small bills for tips and purchases in town.

> **Watch out:** the islands have limited medical facilities, and a serious problem means evacuation to the mainland or further. Your provincial plan won't cover it, so buy travel medical insurance with evacuation cover. Over 60? See [cruise insurance for seniors](/articles/cruise-insurance-seniors-canada-pre-existing/).

## Cruise or land-based trip?
You can also stay in hotels on Santa Cruz, San Cristóbal or Isabela and take day boats to nearby islands. It's usually cheaper and better for anyone prone to seasickness. The trade-off is reach: day boats can't get to the more remote islands, and you spend more time commuting by boat and less time at visitor sites. For most first-timers with the budget, a cruise shows far more of the archipelago in the same week.

## Who this trip suits
It's ideal for keen wildlife watchers, photographers and snorkellers who are happy with early starts and active days. It's harder for anyone with limited mobility: wet landings mean stepping from a zodiac into shallow surf, and trails cross uneven lava. Small yachts can roll, so read about [seasickness at sea](/articles/seasickness-how-bad-really/) if you're prone.

## Planning a Galápagos expedition properly
Choose your ship size first, then your season, then your flights, with a mainland buffer night at each end. Check exactly what the fare includes, buy insurance early, and pack reef-safe sunscreen and a good hat for the equator.
