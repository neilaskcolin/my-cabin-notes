A deck plan is the most useful booking tool most first-timers ignore. Ten minutes with it tells you more about a cabin than any category name, and it's free on every cruise line's website.

## Start with the side profile
Most lines show a side view of the whole ship with each deck labelled. Use it to see the ship's layers at a glance:

- **Lower decks:** the theatre, main dining rooms, shops and guest services, often with a promenade deck and lifeboats along the side.
- **Middle decks:** mostly cabins.
- **Top decks:** pools, the buffet, the spa, sports courts and sometimes suites.

The best cabin decks are the ones sandwiched between other cabin decks, with no public spaces directly above or below.

## Reading cabin numbers and categories
Cabin numbers usually begin with the deck number, so 8320 is on deck 8. The remaining digits often run from forward to aft, and on many ships odd and even numbers sit on opposite sides. Once you've cracked your ship's pattern, you can place any cabin from its number alone.

Each colour on the plan is a category, often with a code such as 4D or B2. Lines split broad categories into sub-categories priced by location, so a midship balcony on a high deck usually costs more than the same balcony aft. Finding a cheaper sub-category in a decent spot is one of the easiest savings there is.

## What do the symbols mean?
Every ship's legend is slightly different, but these are the common ones.

| Symbol or feature | Usually means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Wheelchair icon | Accessible cabin | Limited numbers; book early (see [accessible cruising](/articles/accessible-cruising-mobility-ships/)) |
| Star, circle or triangle by a number | Third or fourth berth: sofa bed or pull-down bunk | Needed for families; can eat floor space |
| Bracket or arrow joining two cabins | Connecting door | Great for families, noisier for everyone else |
| Shaded shapes along the hull | Lifeboats | Cabins just above may have partly blocked views |
| Blank or grey areas | Crew, storage or service spaces | Can mean early-morning noise next door |
| Elevator and stair icons | Elevator lobbies and stairwells | Convenient, but busy corridors |

> **Watch out:** deck plans on third-party sites can be out of date after a ship's refit, when venues move or cabins are added. Check the cruise line's current plan for your sailing date, and confirm anything important before you pay.

## A step-by-step check for any cabin
1. Find the cabin on its deck and note whether it's forward, midship or aft.
2. Open the deck directly above. Look for pools, the buffet, the jogging track or a nightclub.
3. Open the deck directly below. Look for the theatre, galley, show lounge or casino.
4. Check its neighbours on the same deck: elevator lobby, crew door, laundry room or stairwell.
5. Read the legend for berth, connecting door or obstructed-view symbols.
6. Look for photos or a 360-degree view of that exact category.
7. Note the cabin number and ask for it by number when you book.

## Special cases worth spotting
**Hump cabins** sit where the hull bulges midship and can have bigger balconies. **Aft-facing balconies** look straight over the wake and are often deeper, but can catch vibration. **Forward cabins** feel more motion in swell, worth knowing if you're prone to seasickness (see [how bad seasickness really is](/articles/seasickness-how-bad-really/)).
