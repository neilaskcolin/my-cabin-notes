The contract of carriage is the legal agreement between you and the cruise line, and you accept it when you book and again at online check-in. Most of it is routine, but a handful of clauses decide what happens when ports change, when you cancel, or when something goes wrong.

## The clauses that affect ordinary passengers
| Clause | What it typically says | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Itinerary changes | The line can change, skip or reorder ports for weather, safety or operational reasons | A missed port usually earns a refund of that port's fees, not compensation |
| Cancellation schedule | The share of your fare kept at each stage after final payment | The most useful table to read before you book |
| Baggage and valuables | Liability is capped, often at a few hundred US dollars per passenger | Insure valuables; don't pack jewellery in checked bags |
| Medical care | The ship's medical staff are treated as independent of the line, and treatment is charged to you | You need travel medical insurance |
| Shore excursions | Tour operators are independent contractors | The line's responsibility is limited even for tours it sells |
| Conduct and boarding | Age, pregnancy, health and behaviour rules; the right to refuse boarding or disembark you | Breaking rules can end your cruise with no refund |
| Claims and courts | Short notice periods, a named court and often arbitration or a class action waiver | Deadlines can expire before you realise |

## Why the deadlines matter most
Contracts for US-based lines commonly require written notice of an injury claim within six months and a lawsuit within one year, which US maritime law permits. Other claims may have even shorter windows or must go to arbitration.

> **Watch out:** most of these contracts name a specific US court, commonly in Florida, California or Washington state depending on the line, and say they are governed by US maritime law. A Canadian passenger with a serious claim may not be able to sue at home, and the clock starts on the day of the incident. Talk to a lawyer early rather than after months of emails.

## Itinerary changes and missed ports
This is the clause people meet most often. Weather in the Inside Passage, a closed tender port in the Caribbean or a mechanical issue can all change your route. The contract lets the line do this without paying compensation, though lines often refund the port's fees and sometimes add onboard credit as goodwill. If the whole cruise is cancelled, different rules apply; see [what happens if the line cancels your cruise](/articles/what-happens-cruise-cancelled-by-line/).

## How to read it in ten minutes
1. **Open the version for your booking.** It is linked from your confirmation; save a copy as a PDF, since terms can change for later bookings.
2. **Search for "cancel".** Note the penalty schedule and your final payment date. [Deposits and final payment](/articles/cruise-deposits-final-payment/) explains the timeline.
3. **Search for "itinerary"** to see what the line promises if ports change.
4. **Search for "baggage" and "valuables"** to find the liability cap.
5. **Search for "medical"** to see how onboard care is billed. [Cruise medical care costs](/articles/cruise-medical-care-costs/) shows typical charges.
6. **Search for "notice" or "suit"** to find claim deadlines and the named court.

## Where Canadian protections fit in
Provincial consumer protection laws may offer limited help against a cruise line based outside Canada, so don't rely on them. Your practical protections are travel insurance, paying by credit card, and, if you used a registered Canadian travel seller, any provincial compensation fund that applies. [Travel insurance vs cruise line protection](/articles/travel-insurance-vs-cruise-protection/) compares the cover options.

Most big lines also signed up to the cruise industry's Passenger Bill of Rights, adopted in 2013, which commits them to basics such as refunds if a voyage is cancelled for mechanical failure. It sits alongside the contract rather than replacing it.

## Using the contract sensibly
Treat every limit in the contract as a pointer to what your insurance should cover. Read the cancellation schedule before you book, save a copy of the terms, and if something serious happens on board, note the date and act quickly.
