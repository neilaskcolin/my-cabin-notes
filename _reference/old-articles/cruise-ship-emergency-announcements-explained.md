Almost every announcement you'll hear on a cruise is routine, and the coded ones are for crew, not for you. There's one signal that means act now: seven or more short blasts followed by one long blast. Everything else either needs nothing from you or comes with plain instructions.

## What you'll hear and what it means
Wording differs by line, and lines don't publish their code lists, so treat the examples here as commonly reported rather than universal.

| What you hear | Usually means | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Midday message from the bridge | Position, weather, speed | Nothing |
| "All aboard" reminders in port | Final boarding time approaching | Head back if you're ashore |
| "Crew drill, this is a drill" | Training while in port | Nothing, unless told otherwise |
| "Alpha, Alpha, Alpha" plus a location | Medical team called | Keep the corridor clear |
| "Bravo, Bravo, Bravo" plus a location | Fire or smoke being checked | Stay away from that area |
| "Oscar, Oscar, Oscar" | Person overboard | Stay put; the ship may turn sharply |
| Seven short blasts and one long | General emergency alarm | Go to your muster station |

## Why ships use codes at all
A coded call sends the right crew team to the right place in seconds without 4,000 people heading to look. Most "Alpha" calls are a passenger who has fainted or fallen. Most "Bravo" calls are a smoke detector set off by a hair dryer or a galley, checked and cleared within minutes. You may hear a follow-up saying the situation has been resolved.

## The crew drills you'll hear in port
Crews practise emergency roles regularly, typically on port days when many passengers are ashore. You'll hear alarms, announcements and see crew in life jackets heading to stations. The announcement will say it's a crew drill, and passengers just carry on. Some drills ask guests to avoid certain stairwells for a short time.

## The one signal that means act
The general emergency alarm is the same internationally, and it's the sound played at your muster briefing. If you hear it:

1. Stay calm and listen for any announcement from the bridge.
2. Take medication, a warm layer and your cruise card if they're within reach.
3. Walk to your muster station by the stairs, not the lifts.
4. Check in with crew there and follow their instructions.

You don't need to bring a life jacket on most ships, since they're stored at muster stations. The full process is explained in [what actually happens at the muster drill](/articles/cruise-ship-safety-drill-what-happens/).

> **Good to know:** an order to abandon ship is only ever given verbally by the captain, never by an alarm alone. Going to your muster station is a precaution that keeps everyone accounted for.

## When announcements are hard to hear
Routine announcements often aren't broadcast inside cabins, but emergency signals are. If you have hearing loss, tell guest services on day one; many ships provide visual or vibrating alert kits (see [cruising with hearing or vision impairments](/articles/cruising-hearing-vision-impairments/)). For broader reassurance, [how safe cruise ships really are](/articles/whats-safe-cruise-ships-honest-answer/) gives an honest answer.

## Sailing with confidence
Learn the alarm, find your muster station, and let the crew handle the codes. An unfamiliar announcement is almost always someone else's job.
