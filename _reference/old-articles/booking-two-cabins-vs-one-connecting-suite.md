Two standard connecting cabins are usually the cheaper and roomier choice for a family, with two bathrooms and a door you can close at bedtime. A suite earns its premium only when the gap is modest and you will genuinely use its perks. The only way to know is to price both, all-in, for your sailing.

For the broader decision of space and lifestyle, see [suite vs two balcony cabins](/articles/suite-vs-two-balcony-cabins/). This page focuses on the numbers.

## A worked example: family of four, 7 nights
These are illustrative figures for a mainstream line at recent prices. Your sailing will differ, so treat them as a template.

| Cost, 7 nights | Two connecting balconies | One family suite |
|---|---|---|
| Fares | US$5,600 (US$1,400 avg per person) | US$6,600 (US$1,650 avg per person) |
| Taxes and port fees | Same per person | Same per person |
| Gratuities | About US$510–560 | About US$580–640 (suite rates are higher on many lines) |
| Bathrooms | Two | One, sometimes one and a half |
| Balconies | Two | One, larger |
| Space | Two separate rooms with a connecting door | One larger open space |
| Perks | None extra | Priority boarding, possibly lounge or dining |
| **Approximate total, before taxes** | **US$6,100–6,200 (roughly C$8,300–8,600)** | **US$7,180–7,240 (roughly C$9,800–10,100)** |

In this example the two cabins save about US$1,075, roughly C$1,450–1,500. The question becomes whether the suite's perks are worth that to your family.

> **Good to know:** when four people share one cabin, the third and fourth guests often pay a lower fare. With two cabins, each has two full-fare guests. That is why the gap is sometimes smaller than you would expect, and why it is worth pricing rather than assuming.

## Putting a value on suite perks
Perks only count if you would otherwise pay for them or they solve a real problem. Ask yourself:

- **Included specialty dining:** would you have booked two or three specialty dinners anyway? At US$40–70 per adult per dinner, that can be several hundred dollars.
- **Priority boarding and disembarkation:** valuable with small children or tight flights home, worth little otherwise.
- **Suite lounge or concierge:** nice, but many families with young kids barely use it.
- **Bigger single balcony:** great for Alaska scenery, less important on a port-heavy Caribbean week.

If honest valuation of the perks gets close to the price gap, the suite can make sense. If not, the two cabins win.

## When two cabins clearly win
- Teenagers or adult children who want their own space.
- Families who all need the bathroom at 7 a.m.
- Light sleepers and early risers on different schedules.
- Grandparents travelling with grandchildren. See [connecting cabins vs one big cabin](/articles/connecting-cabins-one-big-cabin-families/).

### And when the suite wins
- A promotion narrows the gap to a few hundred dollars.
- Young children who will not sleep in a separate room anyway.
- You would buy the dining or drinks the suite includes.

## The third option for big families: a suite plus a cabin
Families of six or more sometimes book a suite that connects to a standard balcony next door. The adults get the suite and its perks, the kids get their own bathroom, and the perks may extend only to suite guests. It can be the best of both, but these pairings are scarce and sell early. [Multigenerational cruise cabin planning](/articles/multigenerational-cruise-planning-cabins/) covers larger layouts.

## Booking checklist
- Confirm both cabins genuinely connect, not just sit side by side.
- Check that each cabin has an adult assigned to it if your line requires one.
- Compare gratuity rates, since suite guests often pay more per day.
- Ask whether third and fourth guest pricing applies on your sailing.
