Neither cabin is the "right" one. The answer depends mostly on where you are sailing and how much time you will spend in the room. Port-heavy Caribbean week: inside, and spend the difference ashore. Alaska or a sea-day-heavy route: balcony, if you can afford it.

## What you actually get
| | Inside | Oceanview | Balcony |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical size | Roughly 140–180 sq ft | Similar or slightly larger | Roughly 170–220 sq ft plus a private deck |
| Natural light | None | Window that does not open | Glass door to your balcony |
| Sleep | Pitch dark, very quiet | Light unless curtains are good | Light unless curtains are good |
| Price on a 7-night sailing | Lowest | A modest step up | Often US$300–900 more per cabin than inside |
| Best for | Port-heavy trips, tight budgets | People who want daylight | Scenic routes and sea days |

Sizes and prices vary a lot by ship. Some Royal Caribbean ships offer inside cabins with a "virtual balcony" screen or a window over an indoor promenade, which helps if you dislike windowless rooms. The [cabin types explained](/articles/cruise-cabins-explained-inside-oceanview-balcony-suite/) page goes deeper.

## When an inside cabin makes sense
On a Caribbean run from Fort Lauderdale or Miami with a port almost every day, your cabin is for sleeping and showering. Everything else happens on deck or ashore.
- You save hundreds of dollars that can pay for excursions, a specialty dinner or a better flight.
- The darkness makes for excellent sleep, especially after late nights.
- A midship inside cabin on a low deck is one of the steadiest places on the ship for anyone prone to seasickness.

> **Quick math:** a US$600 difference on a 7-night cruise is about US$86 a night, or roughly C$115–120. Ask yourself whether you would pay that each night for the view and the outdoor space.

## When a balcony earns its price
Some itineraries put the show outside your window for hours at a time: Alaska's Inside Passage, glacier days, Norwegian fjords and Panama Canal transits.

### Alaska from Vancouver or Seattle
This is the strongest case. On glacier days the open decks fill up, and a balcony lets you watch in comfort. Morning coffee in a fleece as mountains slide past is a memory many people book for. For a couple, an Alaska balcony often costs US$1,000–1,800 more than an inside, so a May or September sailing helps. Read [the best cabins for Alaska scenery](/articles/best-cabins-alaska-cruise-scenery/) before choosing a side or deck.

### Sea-day-heavy sailings
On a Hawaii sailing from Vancouver, a transatlantic or a Panama Canal cruise, a private outdoor space makes long sea days more relaxing.

> **Watch out:** smoking is banned on balconies on the major lines, and balcony dividers are not soundproof. If you want privacy, avoid cabins below the pool deck or next to lifts.

## The oceanview compromise
An oceanview gives you daylight and a sense of where you are for a smaller premium than a balcony. It suits people who feel closed in by an inside cabin but would not use outdoor furniture. On some sailings, oceanview prices sit so close to balcony prices that the balcony becomes the better buy.

## Two Canadian examples
- **A Toronto family of four** on a 7-night Western Caribbean sailing books two connecting inside cabins and puts the saving towards snorkelling in Cozumel and a beach day in Roatán.
- **A Kelowna couple** celebrating an anniversary on a 7-night Alaska sailing in May picks a midship balcony on the scenic side and skips the drink package to make it fit.

## Price both before deciding
Balcony premiums can swing by hundreds of dollars between neighbouring sailings, and a sale occasionally shrinks the gap to almost nothing. Price inside, oceanview and balcony on your actual dates, including any [guarantee cabin](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/) fares, before assuming the balcony is out of reach.
