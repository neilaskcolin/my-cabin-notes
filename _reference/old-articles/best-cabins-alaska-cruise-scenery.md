For Alaska, the best scenery cabin is a balcony on a mid-to-upper deck, midship or aft, with a clear view confirmed on the deck plan. Category gets you most of the way; the specific cabin number does the rest.

## Is a balcony worth it on an Alaska cruise?

Alaska is the itinerary where the balcony premium makes the most sense. A typical week includes a full glacier day, hours of scenic cruising through narrow channels, and long evenings of daylight in June and July. With your own outdoor space you can watch with a coffee in a dressing gown instead of competing for rail space in a parka.

> **Quick math:** at recent prices, a balcony on a 7-night Alaska sailing often costs around US$500–900 more per person than an inside cabin, or roughly C$1,350–2,450 more per couple. Spread over the scenic days, that is the figure to weigh against what you would spend upgrading anything else.

If that premium means cutting your only excursion, it may not be worth it. The [inside vs balcony comparison](/articles/inside-vs-balcony-first-cruise/) walks through that trade-off.

## Which deck and position should you pick?

### Deck height

Higher decks see over the ship's own superstructure and give wider views of mountain ranges. Lower balcony decks sit closer to the water, which suits wildlife spotting, since a humpback's blow or a sea otter is easier to pick out from nearer the surface. A mid-to-upper deck usually balances both. Avoid cabins directly below the pool deck or buffet if you want quiet early mornings; the [quietest cabins guide](/articles/quietest-cabins-where-to-book/) covers what to steer clear of.

### Midship, forward or aft

Midship moves least, which matters only if someone is motion-sensitive. Forward cabins catch more wind on scenic days. Aft-facing cabins are the hidden gem: from the stern you see the channel unfolding behind you on both sides, and many aft balconies are deeper than standard ones.

### Port or starboard

On a round trip from Vancouver the views even out, because you pass the same coast going and coming. On a one-way sailing, the coast-facing side gets more shoreline in each direction. Ships also turn at the big glaciers so both sides get a look. The full argument is in [which side of the ship is best for Alaska](/articles/which-side-of-ship-alaska/).

## The obstruction trap

Lifeboats hang along the sides of most big ships, and the balconies just above or beside them can lose much of the view. Lines usually sell these as obstructed or partial-view, but not every overhang or pillar is labelled. Some ships also use solid metal panels on the lower part of the railing, so you see less when seated than when standing.

> **Good to know:** a guarantee cabin lets the line choose your cabin for a lower price, which is fine on a Caribbean trip but risky here, since you could land a blocked view. See [what a guarantee cabin is](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/) before you take one for Alaska.

The fix is simple: check the exact cabin number on the [deck plan](/articles/reading-cruise-ship-deck-plans/), look for lifeboat symbols on the deck below, and search the cabin number online for photos taken from that balcony.

## Book early for the good ones

Aft balconies, corner suites and unobstructed mid-deck cabins on Alaska sailings tend to go first, especially for July and August, when Canadian school holidays add demand. Booking early gives you the pick; booking late means taking what is left. ## Category versus cabin number

Two balconies at the same price can give completely different weeks: one with a clear view of every fjord, another staring at the top of a lifeboat. Once you have chosen the category, spend ten minutes on the specific number. It is the cheapest upgrade available on an Alaska cruise.
