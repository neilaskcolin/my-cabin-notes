The quietest cabin on almost any ship is midship, on a deck sandwiched between other cabin decks, and away from elevators, service doors and the very front and back of the ship. Ten minutes with the deck plan before booking is the best sleep insurance you can buy.

## The sandwich rule
Cabins above you and cabins below you are the quietest neighbours on a ship, because everyone is asleep at roughly the same time. Most bad nights trace back to breaking this rule.

Common culprits include deck chairs dragged across the pool deck at 6 a.m., bass from a theatre or nightclub coming up through the floor, and early-morning carts in a galley or buffet above.

## What to check above, below and beside

| Where | Noisy neighbours to avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Above | Pool deck, lido buffet, gym, jogging track, nightclub | Furniture, footsteps, early set-up, late music |
| Below | Theatre, lounges, casino, galley | Bass and late shows, kitchen work through the night |
| Beside | Elevator lobby, stairwell, crew door, laundry room | Traffic, chimes and doors until late |
| Same cabin | Connecting door you don't need | Sound passes easily around the door |
| Forward, low decks | Anchor and mooring area, bow thrusters | Loud clanking when anchoring or docking |
| Aft, low decks | Propellers and mooring lines | Vibration and docking noise |

Cabins at the end of a short corridor, or in the middle of a long run of cabins between stair towers, are usually the quietest on the deck.

## How to read the deck plan in five steps
1. Find your candidate cabin on the cruise line's deck plan, not a third-party map that may be out of date.
2. Look at the deck directly above. Is it all cabins, or is there a pool, buffet or gym over your ceiling?
3. Look at the deck directly below. Check for theatres, bars, the casino or a galley.
4. Look along your corridor for elevators, stairs, laundry rooms and doors marked for crew.
5. Note how close you are to the bow or stern. Midship is best for both noise and motion.

If you are new to deck plans, [how to read a cruise ship deck plan](/articles/reading-cruise-ship-deck-plans/) explains the symbols.

> **Good to know:** midship, mid-height cabins also move the least in rough water, so the quietest location is often the steadiest too. That is why experienced cruisers ask for the same few cabin numbers on a ship year after year.

## Inside, balcony or suite?
Inside cabins are often quietest of all, because they have no outside wall and are usually surrounded by other cabins. They are also darkest, which helps light sleepers.

Balconies let in wind, waves and sometimes neighbours chatting outside. On Alaska sailings, early glacier commentary can carry along the side of the ship. Suites are often near the top of the ship, close to the pool deck or forward, so check them as carefully as any cabin.

## Why guarantee fares are risky for light sleepers
A guarantee fare lets the cruise line choose your cabin, often for a lower price. You might land a lovely quiet cabin, or one under the buffet. If quiet matters, book a specific cabin number. See [guarantee cabins and whether to book one](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/).

## If you end up with a noisy cabin anyway
Report it to guest services on the first night, with the times the noise happens. Ask about moving to another cabin. Earplugs and a white-noise app are worth packing regardless. [What to do about a noisy cabin](/articles/cruise-cabin-noise-complaints-what-to-do/) covers your options and what compensation to expect.

A quiet cabin on a party ship can still be noisy, especially on deck. If calm is the goal for the whole trip, start with [quiet adult-focused ships without waterslides](/articles/quiet-ships-without-waterslides/), then apply the sandwich rule to choose your cabin.
