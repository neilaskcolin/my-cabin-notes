Alaska is more accessible than its rugged image suggests, because the main cruise towns are small and built along flat waterfronts. What trips people up is less the towns themselves than how you get ashore and onto tour transport. Here is what each port realistically offers.

## Before you pick excursions: three Alaska-specific hurdles
### Tides change the gangway angle
Southeast Alaska has big tides, often several metres between high and low water. At low tide, gangways and floating-dock ramps can be steep, both off the ship and onto tour boats. Ask the crew about timing, and avoid tours that board small boats at low tide if ramps are a problem.

### Tender ports
A few stops, such as Sitka on some ships and Haines, may use tenders. Whether a wheelchair can transfer depends on the sea state and the chair. The [cruise ship tenders explained](/articles/cruise-ship-tenders-explained/) page covers how this works.

### Docks outside town
Some ships berth a few kilometres from downtown, served by shuttle buses. Ask whether those shuttles have lifts or ramps.

> **Watch out:** "accessible" on an excursion listing often means "limited walking", not "wheelchair accessible". Ask specifically whether the vehicle has a lift and whether you must transfer from your chair.

## Ketchikan
Ships usually dock along the downtown waterfront, which is fairly flat. Within a short roll you have the Southeast Alaska Discovery Center, the Totem Heritage Center and shops along the front. Creek Street's boardwalk is charming but has slopes and uneven planks, so go with a companion.

For excursions, boat trips to Misty Fjords on larger catamarans are usually more manageable than floatplanes, which require climbing onto a float. Totem parks such as Saxman and Totem Bight have gravel paths of varying quality, so ask the operator. [Ketchikan on your own](/articles/ketchikan-free-walking-plan/) maps the downtown highlights.

## Juneau
Juneau has the strongest list of accessible options:
- **Mount Roberts Tramway:** starts beside the cruise docks, and the cars and top station are built for wheelchairs. Clouds can hide the view, so go when it is clear.
- **Mendenhall Glacier:** the visitor centre has ramps and an elevator, and the short paved Photo Point trail gives a good glacier view. The Steep Creek boardwalk is a good place to watch for black bears and salmon in late summer.
- **Whale watching:** some larger boats have an accessible main cabin, but boarding ramps at the harbour depend on the tide.

See [Juneau shore excursions ranked by value](/articles/juneau-shore-excursions-ranked/) for the wider picture.

## Skagway
Downtown sits on a flat valley floor, with wooden boardwalks along Broadway that are mostly level but uneven in places. Depending on the dock, town can be a fair distance away, and a shuttle helps.

The White Pass & Yukon Route railway offers wheelchair-accessible cars with a lift, and the trip needs no walking once aboard. Request accessible seating when you book, either through the ship or directly with the railway. [Is the White Pass railway worth it](/articles/skagway-white-pass-railway-worth-it/) covers the route.

## Icy Strait Point
This is a purpose-built port with paved paths around the restored cannery, shops and restaurants close to the dock. The zipline and many bear-viewing tours are not suitable for wheelchair users, but the waterfront and cultural presentations generally are. [What's at Icy Strait Point](/articles/icy-strait-point-whats-there/) explains the layout.

## Glacier days: accessible by default
Scenic cruising needs nothing from you except a good viewing spot. Indoor observation lounges and accessible public decks forward work well. In Glacier Bay, National Park Service rangers usually come aboard to give talks. Book an [accessible cabin with a good view](/articles/wheelchair-accessible-alaska-cruises/) early, since they sell out.

## How to plan it, in order
1. Check the itinerary for tender ports and docks outside town.
2. Contact the cruise line's accessibility department with your chair or scooter's dimensions and weight. [Accessible cruising on mobility-friendly ships](/articles/accessible-cruising-mobility-ships/) covers cabins and onboard help.
3. For each port, shortlist one or two excursions and ask the operator about lifts, transfers and boarding ramps.
4. If renting a scooter, arrange delivery to the ship through a specialist supplier.
5. Bring documents for any equipment, plus a basic repair kit.

> **Canadian tip:** your provincial health plan covers very little in the US or on the ship. Make sure your travel insurance covers mobility equipment loss or damage, not just medical care.
