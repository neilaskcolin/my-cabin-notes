A charity cruise works best as two clearly separate pieces: an ordinary group booking where everyone pays for their own trip, and a fundraising layer people can see and trust. Mixing the two, for example by quietly padding fares, is where well-meant trips run into trouble.

## How does a charity cruise actually work?
The cruise itself is a standard group block. Each guest books and pays for their own cabin, picking whatever category they like, as in [how to pick your first cruise cabin](/articles/pick-first-cruise-cabin/). The group reaches perk level at about eight cabins, explained in [how the 8-cabin rule works](/articles/group-cruise-8-cabin-rule/).

The fundraising sits on top. It's money given to the cause, collected and accounted for separately, with its own clear rules.

## Fundraising structures that work
- **A per-cabin or per-person contribution.** For example, a suggested donation of C$100 per cabin, disclosed upfront and paid to the charity directly rather than bundled into the fare.
- **The organiser's free berth, given to the cause.** The tour conductor credit earned at group level can be auctioned or raffled, with the proceeds donated. See [what organisers get free](/articles/group-cruise-perks-organiser/).
- **An onboard event.** A private cocktail hour or dinner using the group's event allowance, with a ticket or a donation ask attached.
- **Sponsored participants.** Guests raise pledges from friends and family ahead of the trip, with the money going straight to the charity.

> **Example:** a 12-cabin group asks for a C$100 donation per cabin (C$1,200), auctions the organiser's free berth at a pre-trip gala for C$1,500, and runs an onboard trivia night that raises another C$400. That's C$3,100 for the cause, with every fare paid separately.

## Tax receipts and the CRA
> **Canadian tip:** only a registered charity can issue an official donation receipt, and it must be the charity, not the organiser, who issues it. A cruise fare is payment for a holiday, so it can't be receipted. Where a donor gets something back, like an auction prize or a dinner, CRA rules reduce the receiptable amount by its value. Ask the charity's treasurer how they want it handled.

If you're raising money for a cause that isn't a registered charity, such as a family in need or a local team, be clear that there are no tax receipts.

## Raffles, auctions and events on board
In Canada, raffles and lotteries usually need a licence from the provincial gaming regulator, and charities are used to applying for these. On a ship, the cruise line's rules apply. Ask the line in writing before planning any raffle, auction or paid event on board, or hold the draw ashore before you sail.

> **Watch out:** never collect donations through the same payments as cruise fares. If a guest cancels, or the cruise line changes something, untangling which money was a fare and which was a gift becomes a headache that can damage trust.

## Setting it up, step by step
1. **Agree terms with the charity**, including written permission to use its name and logo. Most charities have rules about this.
2. **Choose your fundraising model** and a realistic target.
3. **Open the group block** with a sailing and price range that suits your supporters.
4. **Publish a one-page breakdown** showing what's the fare, what's the donation, and where each goes.
5. **Collect donations through the charity's own donation page** or its treasurer, separate from cruise payments.
6. **Share the result** after the trip, with thanks and a final total.

## Choosing a sailing that suits supporters
A 4–7 night sailing in shoulder season keeps fares within reach, which matters when you're also asking people to give. Alaska from Vancouver in May or September works well for Western Canadian supporters, and a short Caribbean sailing suits Ontario and Quebec groups in winter. ## Setting up the cruise side properly
The fundraising structure is yours to design. The cruise side should follow standard group practice: one block, clear payment dates, and perks claimed and used as promised. [Group cruise deadlines](/articles/group-cruise-deadlines-deposits/) covers the dates to publish, and [group cruise insurance](/articles/group-cruise-insurance-one-policy-everyone/) explains why each guest should still buy their own cover.
