For travellers with limited mobility, a cruise solves the hardest parts of a trip. You unpack once, your hotel moves with you, dinner is an elevator ride away, and the scenery comes to the window. What makes or breaks it is the order you book things in.

## Why cruise ships suit limited mobility
Modern ships are built with elevators to every passenger deck, step-free routes between most public areas, wheelchair spaces in theatres, and, on many newer ships, pool lifts. There are no daily hotel changes or coach transfers to manage. For couples where one partner's mobility now shapes every trip, a cruise often reopens places that felt out of reach.

Ship age matters. Newer, larger ships are generally the most accessible. Older small ships, expedition vessels and river cruise boats can have narrow doors, steep gangways or decks that the elevator doesn't reach.

## What types of accessible cabin are there?
Lines use different names, so ask exactly what each cabin includes.

| Cabin type | Typical features | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Fully accessible | Wide door, roll-in shower with fold-down seat, grab bars, lowered fixtures, turning space | Full-time wheelchair and scooter users |
| Ambulatory accessible | Grab bars and shower seat, but a standard door and less floor space | People who walk with a cane or walker |
| Standard cabin with aids | Bed rails, shower stool or raised toilet seat on request | Mild mobility limits |
| Hearing or vision support | Visual alarms, bed shaker kits, Braille signage | See [cruising with hearing or vision loss](/articles/cruising-hearing-vision-impairments/) |

> **Good to know:** many lines limit accessible cabins to guests who need the features, and some ask you to confirm this when booking. Most also say crew cannot provide personal care, so anyone who needs help with dressing or transfers should sail with a companion.

## The booking order that matters
1. **Book the accessible cabin first.** There are only a handful per ship, they release when the sailing opens, and they can be gone months out.
2. **Filter the itinerary for docked ports.** Tender ports can rule out powered chairs and scooters entirely. Santorini, for example, is a tender port with a cable car up the cliff.
3. **Send your needs to the line in writing.** Include equipment dimensions and weight, shower type, and any help needed at embarkation. Keep the confirmation.
4. **Sort the equipment.** Rent a scooter delivered to your cabin, or measure your own against the cabin door width.
5. **Book flights and transfers,** including terminal assistance at the port.
6. **Buy insurance** that covers your pre-existing conditions and your mobility equipment. [Cruise insurance for seniors in Canada](/articles/cruise-insurance-seniors-canada-pre-existing/) explains the age and condition rules.

### Flying with a wheelchair or scooter from Canada
Under Canadian accessible transportation rules, large Canadian airlines carry mobility aids at no charge, and for domestic flights you may qualify for a free seat for a required attendant. Scooters with lithium batteries need advance approval and have battery size limits, so call the airline well before you fly.

> **Canadian tip:** charging a scooter is usually fine from a standard cabin outlet, but most lines ban surge-protected power bars. Read [the rules on extension cords](/articles/extension-cord-cruise-ship-rules/) before you pack one.

## Which itineraries work best?
- **Alaska** is a strong choice, as glacier days are viewed from the ship. See [wheelchair-accessible Alaska cruises from Vancouver](/articles/wheelchair-accessible-alaska-cruises/).
- **Transatlantic crossings and Hawaii sailings from Vancouver** have many sea days, though Kona in Hawaii is usually a tender port.
- **Caribbean** routes are mixed: some private islands dock, others tender.
- **New England and Canada** cruises dock in Halifax, with a flat waterfront, and Quebec City, where the upper town is steep.
- **European** ports often mean cobbles, hills and tenders, so check each call carefully.
- **River cruises** can be harder than ocean ships, because boats often moor side by side and you may need to cross another vessel to reach shore. Compare in [river cruise vs ocean cruise](/articles/river-cruise-vs-ocean-cruise-over-50/).

## Honest limits
Accessible shore excursions are clearly labelled in major ports but thin out at small tender stops. "Accessible" can mean anything from a lift-equipped coach to "limited walking", so ask exactly what's involved. Weather can also cancel tender operations for everyone. Get the specifics before paying a deposit rather than arguing about them after.
