On most lines, a group starts at 8 cabins, or roughly 16 guests sharing. Below that you're a collection of individual bookings. At 8 and above, you're a group with a held rate, perks for the organiser and one point of contact for everyone.

## Why 8 cabins is the magic number
Cruise lines hold group space in blocks that make commercial sense for them, and 8 cabins is the most common threshold. Some lines start group pricing at 5 cabins and others at 10, so check the line's group terms before you start counting.

The headline perk is the **tour conductor credit**: typically one free berth for every 16 paying guests. That's one person's cruise fare, not a whole cabin. Taxes, port fees and gratuities are still owed.

## What different group sizes typically get
This is the common pattern. Exact terms vary by line, ship and sailing, so confirm the details in your group contract.

| Cabins booked together | Typical result |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | Individual bookings; you might share a promotion or a dinner table request |
| 5–7 | A group rate on some lines, sometimes with small extras; usually no free berth |
| 8 (about 16 paying guests) | Group status, a held rate, and one tour conductor credit |
| 16 (about 32 paying guests) | Two tour conductor credits, with more leverage for extras |
| 25 or more | Richer amenities, private events and better negotiating room |

> **Quick math:** if 16 paying guests book at a fare of US$1,200 each, the group earns one credit worth about US$1,200 (roughly C$1,650) off one person's fare. The organiser's travelling companion still pays their own fare, and both still pay taxes and gratuities.

## How the block works behind the scenes
One person, or one booking agency, opens a block: a set number of cabins held at a group rate for a period. Guests then book into it, each choosing their own cabin category and paying their own deposit. Mixing categories is fine. The uncle in an inside cabin and the honeymooners in a suite are both part of the same block.

Group rates are usually fixed when the block opens, rather than moving up and down like everyday cruise prices. Unsold cabins can typically be released back to the line by a set date without penalty, which is why a block is low risk to open. The dates that matter are covered in [group cruise deadlines and deposits](/articles/group-cruise-deadlines-deposits/).

## Falling short of 8
Seven cabins usually means no free berth, but a block is still worth opening. Some lines give smaller groups a held rate or small extras, and the perks switch on if an eighth cabin joins before final payment. Keep inviting people until the last date when new cabins can join.

If someone drops out late and the block falls below the threshold, the perks can disappear too. [What happens if someone drops out of a group cruise](/articles/what-if-someone-drops-out-group-cruise/) covers the knock-on effects.

## Common mistakes with group bookings
- **Counting people instead of cabins, or the other way round.** Some lines count cabins, others count paying guests. Know which applies.
- **Letting everyone book separately first.** Bookings can sometimes be moved into a group later, but not always at the same price.
- **Assuming the group rate is always the cheapest.** A public sale can undercut it, so ask whether the group can be repriced or use the promotion.
- **Missing the release date** and paying for cabins nobody wants.
- **Not getting the amenities in writing.** If it isn't in the contract, it may not appear on board.

## Setting one up properly
Opening the block early gives you the best cabin choice and the longest runway to fill it. [What organisers get free](/articles/group-cruise-perks-organiser/) explains how to use the credit, and [negotiating the best cruise group rate](/articles/negotiating-cruise-group-rates/) covers what's open to negotiation. A licensed cruise consultant can open and manage the block at no extra cost over individual bookings.
