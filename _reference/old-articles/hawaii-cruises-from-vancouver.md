Twice a year, ships that spend the summer in Alaska head to or from Hawaii, and Vancouver becomes a starting point for the islands. You get a proper ocean crossing, warm island days at the far end, and, on a round trip, no airport at all.

## How the voyages are built
Ships leaving Alaska in the fall sail from Vancouver across the Pacific, call at several islands, and then either end in Honolulu or turn back to Vancouver. In spring the flow reverses, with one-way voyages from Honolulu ending at Canada Place.

Here is an example of a one-way fall voyage. Ports and order vary by sailing.

| Day | Where |
|---|---|
| 1 | Depart Vancouver, late afternoon |
| 2–6 | At sea crossing the Pacific |
| 7 | Hilo, Big Island |
| 8 | Kailua-Kona, Big Island (often by tender) |
| 9 | Kahului, Maui |
| 10 | Nawiliwili, Kauai |
| 11 | Honolulu, Oahu, often overnight |
| 12 | Disembark in Honolulu and fly home |

Round trips add a second crossing back to Vancouver, so they run roughly 14–18 nights. Because Vancouver is a foreign port under US shipping rules, these itineraries don't need the detour to Mexico that round trips from Los Angeles or San Diego usually make.

## What the sea days are like
The crossing is the heart of the trip. Expect lectures, classes, trivia, the spa and long lunches, with the air warming each day. Leaving Vancouver in October, the first day or two can be cool and grey, so pack layers as well as summer clothes. Clocks go back an hour on several nights, since Hawaii is three hours behind Vancouver in spring and fall.

The North Pacific can be lively, especially late in the fall. If you're prone to seasickness, choose a cabin low and in the middle of the ship, and read [seasickness on a cruise: how bad is it really](/articles/seasickness-how-bad-really/).

> **Canadian tip:** Air Canada and WestJet fly nonstop between Honolulu and Vancouver, with seasonal flights to other Canadian cities. On a one-way fall voyage, book a one-way flight home early; on a spring voyage, fly out to Honolulu a day before the ship leaves.

## Who these sailings suit
- **Retired couples** with two or three weeks free, especially outside school holidays, since these voyages run while Canadian schools are in session.
- **People who dislike flying,** who can take the train, drive or a short flight to Vancouver and sail the rest.
- **Sea-day lovers** who see a five-day crossing as the main attraction (see [enrichment at sea](/articles/enrichment-at-sea-lectures/) for what fills those days).

They suit people less if a week is all you have, or if more than two sea days in a row would bore you. In that case, fly to Honolulu and take an island-focused cruise instead.

## What they cost
These are repositioning voyages, so per-night fares are often lower than the same ship's summer Alaska rates, and balconies can be reasonable for a warm-weather route. [Repositioning cruises explained](/articles/repositioning-cruises-explained/) covers why.

> **Quick math:** on a round trip, a couple skips two return flights to Hawaii. At roughly C$600–1,000 each for a Vancouver–Honolulu return in economy, that's about C$1,200–2,000 saved, which can cover a balcony upgrade.

For a full budget including flights, gratuities and excursions, see [Hawaii cruise cost from Canada](/articles/hawaii-cruise-cost-canada/).

## Planning tips
### Insurance and trip length
A two-to-three week trip with a long ocean crossing makes travel medical insurance essential, and some policies limit trip length. If you're over 60, check stability periods too, as explained in [cruise insurance for seniors](/articles/cruise-insurance-seniors-canada-pre-existing/).

### Booking timing
Only a handful of these voyages run each season, and many go to repeat passengers who book well ahead. Watch for next year's fall departures as soon as they open, and decide early whether you want one-way or round trip.
