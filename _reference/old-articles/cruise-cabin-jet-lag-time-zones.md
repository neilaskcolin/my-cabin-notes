Ships do change time zones, but gently: usually one hour at a time, overnight, with a warning in the daily programme. The bigger trap is that some ships stay on one "ship's time" while the ports keep their own, and the ship's clock is the only one that counts for getting back on board.

## How do cruise ships change the clocks?
When a ship sails into a new zone, the bridge moves the ship's clock forward or back by an hour, almost always at 2 a.m. or so, ahead of a sea day where possible. You'll see it in the daily programme and the app the evening before.

On routes that hop between zones every day or two, lines often pick one ship's time for the whole voyage instead. That keeps dining and show times stable, but it means the clock on board may not match the clock tower ashore.

## Where Canadian cruisers meet time changes

| Itinerary | What changes | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Alaska from Vancouver or Seattle | Alaska is 1 hour behind Pacific time | Most ships switch once in Alaskan waters, then back on the way south |
| Eastern Caribbean from Florida | Islands like St. Maarten and St. Thomas are 1 hour ahead of Florida in winter, the same in summer | Ships often stay on Florida time |
| Western Caribbean from Florida | Cozumel, Jamaica and Grand Cayman are 1 hour behind in summer; Roatán and Belize are 1 hour behind in winter, 2 in summer | Tour pickups are in local time |
| Hawaii from Vancouver | Hawaii is 3 hours behind Vancouver during daylight time | Clocks go back an hour on several nights heading out |
| Transatlantic | 5–6 hours between Florida or New York and Europe | Eastbound loses an hour on several nights |

The Caribbean differences come from daylight saving: many islands don't use it, so their gap with Florida shifts in March and November.

> **Watch out:** all-aboard is always in ship's time. If your Cozumel tour operator says "back at 4" in local time and the ship is on Florida time, you could be an hour later than you think. ### Phones, watches and the ship's network
Smartphones set their own time from the nearest network. In port that may be local time; at sea it may be the ship's maritime cell service or nothing at all. Switch off automatic time zone on your phone at boarding and set it to ship's time, or wear a watch. Keep data roaming off too, as explained in [cruise wifi and phone roaming for Canadians](/articles/cruise-wifi-phone-roaming-canadians/).

## Jet lag on a fly-cruise
The ship's clock changes are mild. The flight to the ship is what knocks people over. Flying from Calgary to Fort Lauderdale moves you two hours ahead, from Vancouver three. For a Mediterranean cruise, Toronto is six hours behind most of Europe and Vancouver nine.

- **North America to Florida:** a pre-cruise hotel night is plenty. You'll feel it most as an early alarm on embarkation day.
- **Canada to Europe:** arrive two nights early, get daylight on your first afternoon, and avoid a long nap. [Flying in the day before your cruise](/articles/fly-day-before-cruise/) covers why a buffer matters.
- **Transatlantic crossings:** the ship does the adjusting for you. Westbound, gaining an hour on several nights is the gentlest way to come home.
- **Hawaii round trips from Vancouver:** you gain three hours going out and lose them coming back, spread across the sea days.

## When clock changes affect your plans
A lost hour the night before an early tender or excursion can make for a groggy start, so check the programme before booking a 7 a.m. tour. If you're working remotely, your colleagues' hours shift relative to yours each time the clock moves, which is covered in [can you work remotely from a cruise ship](/articles/can-you-work-remotely-cruise-ship/).

## The one rule to remember
Trust the ship's clock, not the port's or your phone's. Read the daily programme each evening, set a watch to ship's time, and add a buffer to every all-aboard. For a cruise that starts several time zones from home, an extra night before boarding does more for jet lag than anything you can pack.
