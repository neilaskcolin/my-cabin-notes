Every fare chart lists the same four categories, but the gap between them is bigger than a window. Here's what each one really gives you, roughly what it costs on a typical sailing from Canada, and how to match the cabin to the trip.

## The four cabin categories at a glance
Sizes are typical for mainstream lines and vary by ship. Prices are rough per-person fares for a 7-night Alaska sailing from Vancouver in shoulder season, at recent prices, based on two sharing.

| Category | Typical size | Rough fare per person | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inside | 150–180 sq ft | US$700–1,100 (roughly C$950–1,550) | Port-heavy trips, sound sleepers, tight budgets |
| Oceanview | 170–200 sq ft | US$850–1,400 (roughly C$1,150–1,950) | Daylight without the balcony price |
| Balcony | 200–250 sq ft including the balcony | US$1,200–2,000 (roughly C$1,650–2,800) | Scenic routes, sea days, couples |
| Suite | 300 sq ft to well over 1,000 | US$2,500 and up (roughly C$3,400+) | Space, perks, families, special occasions |

Peak summer and school holidays push all of these higher. Taxes, port fees and gratuities are per person and don't change with category.

## Inside cabins
No window and no natural light, which makes them very dark and very quiet for sleeping. They're the cheapest way onto a ship, and modern ones are cleverly laid out rather than cramped. Some ships offer twists on the idea, such as interior cabins with a "virtual balcony" screen or rooms that look over an indoor promenade.

They work best when the cabin is mainly a bedroom: a week of daily ports in the Caribbean, or a short getaway.

## Oceanview cabins
A window or porthole lets in daylight and lets you check the weather before getting dressed. You can't step outside. Look out for "obstructed view" oceanview cabins, where lifeboats block part of the window; they're cheaper for a reason.

This is a good middle ground for people who find windowless rooms disorienting, or for anyone prone to seasickness who likes seeing the horizon.

## Balcony cabins
A sliding door opens onto a small private deck with two chairs and a little table. It's the most popular category on many modern ships. The balcony earns its price on scenic routes, such as Alaska's Inside Passage or a Norwegian fjords cruise, and on itineraries with several sea days.

Watch the wording: some lines sell cabins where the "balcony" is a window that slides down inside the room, or a narrow French balcony with no seating. On Alaska sailings, side matters too; see [the best cabins for Alaska scenery](/articles/best-cabins-alaska-cruise-scenery/).

## Suites
The widest category on the ship. A junior suite may be a slightly larger balcony cabin with a tub and sofa. Top suites have separate bedrooms, huge decks and butler service. Some lines group their best suites into a private enclave with its own restaurant, lounge and sun deck, such as Norwegian's Haven.

Perks commonly include priority boarding, specialty dining credits or access, better toiletries, and sometimes drinks or wifi. Whether that's worth it is weighed up in [one suite vs two balcony cabins](/articles/suite-vs-two-balcony-cabins/).

## Other cabin types you'll see
- **Studio cabins** for solo travellers, avoiding the single supplement on some lines (see [how to dodge the single supplement](/articles/single-supplement-how-to-dodge/)).
- **Spa cabins**, near the spa with wellness perks.
- **Family cabins** that sleep four to six, compared in [connecting cabins vs one big cabin](/articles/connecting-cabins-one-big-cabin-families/).
- **Accessible cabins** with roll-in showers and wider doors, which should be booked early.
- **Guarantee cabins**, where you choose the category and the line picks the room later, explained in [should you book a guarantee cabin](/articles/guarantee-cabin-should-you-book/).

## How to choose the right category
1. **Start with the itinerary.** Port-heavy weeks favour inside or oceanview; scenery and sea days favour a balcony.
2. **Then pick location.** Midship on a middle deck is steadier and quieter, and [how to pick your first cruise cabin](/articles/pick-first-cruise-cabin/) explains why location can matter more than category.
3. **Compare total trip cost.** Put the saving next to what it could buy, such as an excursion or a drinks budget.
4. **Check the deck plan** for what's above and below before you pay.

> **Canadian tip:** many lines invite upgrade bids after you book, where you offer a price for a higher category. Bids are in US dollars, so convert before you offer; [how cabin upgrade bids work](/articles/cabin-upgrades-bids-explained/) covers the details.

## The short answer
Inside for value, oceanview for daylight, balcony for scenery, suite for space and perks. The price gaps between categories shift from sailing to sailing, so a balcony that's a stretch on one date can be a small step up on another. Compare the same ship across a few dates before deciding.
